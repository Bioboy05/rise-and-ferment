# Glossary of Sourdough Terms

**Active starter** — A starter that has been recently fed and is showing signs of life: bubbles, rising, and a pleasant tangy aroma. This is the state you want before mixing dough.

**Autolyse** — A rest period (usually 30-60 minutes) where flour and water are mixed together before adding starter or salt. This allows the flour to fully hydrate and begins gluten development with zero effort from you.

**Banneton** — A proofing basket (often made of rattan or cane) used to support shaped dough during its final rise. The spiral pattern leaves beautiful rings on the finished loaf. A bowl lined with a floured tea towel works as a budget alternative.

**Batch** — A single session of bread-making, from mixing through baking. Planning your batches around your starter's peak activity gives the best results.

**Bench rest** — A short pause (15-30 minutes) between pre-shaping and final shaping. The dough relaxes during this time, making it easier to handle and shape tightly without tearing.

**Boule** — A round loaf shape, the most common form for sourdough bread. The word comes from French and simply means "ball."

**Bulk fermentation** — The first and longest rise after mixing your dough. This is where most of the flavor develops and the dough gains strength through folds. It typically lasts 4-8 hours at room temperature.

**Coil fold** — A gentle folding technique where you lift the dough from the center and let the edges fold underneath by gravity. Excellent for high-hydration doughs that are too sticky for stretch and folds.

**Cold retard** — See **Retard**.

**Crumb** — The interior texture of your bread. An "open crumb" has large, irregular holes; a "tight crumb" has small, uniform holes. Both are valid — it depends on what you are baking and your personal preference.

**Crust** — The outer shell of the bread, formed by high oven heat. A dark, caramelized crust develops the deepest flavors thanks to the Maillard reaction.

**Discard** — The portion of starter you remove before each feeding. It is not waste — you can use it in pancakes, waffles, crackers, pizza dough, and dozens of other recipes.

**Dough strength** — How well your dough holds its shape. You build strength through folds during bulk fermentation. A dough with good strength will dome up on the counter rather than spreading flat.

**Dutch oven** — A heavy, lidded pot (cast iron or enameled) used to bake sourdough. The lid traps steam during the first phase of baking, giving you maximum oven spring and a crackly crust. The single most impactful piece of equipment for home sourdough baking.

**Ear** — The flap of crust that lifts along a score line during baking. A pronounced ear is a sign of good oven spring, proper shaping, and confident scoring. It is mostly aesthetic — your bread tastes the same without one.

**Elasticity** — The dough's ability to spring back after being stretched. Gluten provides elasticity. If your dough snaps back aggressively, it needs more rest.

**Extensibility** — The dough's ability to stretch without tearing. Autolyse and time improve extensibility. You want a balance of elasticity and extensibility for easy shaping.

**Feed / Feeding** — Adding fresh flour and water to your starter to provide food for the wild yeast and bacteria. A typical ratio is 1:1:1 (starter:flour:water by weight).

**Fermentation** — The metabolic process where wild yeast and bacteria consume sugars in the flour, producing carbon dioxide (which makes bread rise) and organic acids (which give sourdough its flavor).

**Float test** — A quick readiness test: drop a small spoonful of starter into water. If it floats, the starter is full of gas and likely at or near peak activity. Not 100% reliable, but a useful indicator for beginners.

**Fold** — A technique for building dough strength without traditional kneading. You stretch one side of the dough up and over itself, rotate, and repeat. Common types include stretch and fold, coil fold, and lamination.

**Gluten** — A network of proteins (glutenin and gliadin) that forms when flour and water are mixed. Gluten traps gas and gives bread its structure and chew.

**Grigne** — The pattern created by scoring the dough before baking. Beyond being decorative, scoring controls where and how the bread expands in the oven.

**Hooch** — The dark liquid (gray, brown, or black) that forms on top of an unfed starter. It is alcohol produced by hungry yeast and is a sign your starter needs feeding. Simply stir it back in or pour it off and feed.

**Hydration** — The ratio of water to flour, expressed as a percentage. A dough with 500g flour and 375g water is at 75% hydration. Higher hydration generally means a more open crumb but stickier, harder-to-handle dough.

**Inclusion** — Any ingredient added to the dough beyond flour, water, salt, and starter. Seeds, nuts, dried fruit, olives, cheese, herbs — these are all inclusions. Add them during the final set of folds to distribute evenly.

**Inoculation** — The percentage of starter used relative to the total flour in your dough. A lower inoculation (5-10%) means slower fermentation and more complex flavor. A higher inoculation (20%+) means faster fermentation.

**Lame** — A tool used to score bread dough before baking, typically a thin razor blade attached to a handle. Pronounced "LAHM." A sharp razor blade or serrated knife can substitute in a pinch.

**Lamination** — A folding technique where you stretch the dough out very thin on a wet surface (like a large sheet), then fold it back onto itself. Great for incorporating inclusions and building massive dough strength in one move.

**Levain** — A portion of starter that has been fed specifically for a bake, often with a particular flour blend or at a certain ratio. Functionally, it is the same as an active starter — the term is mostly a matter of tradition and specificity.

**Maillard reaction** — The chemical reaction between amino acids and sugars that occurs at high heat, creating the deep brown color and complex flavor of a well-baked crust. It is not the same as caramelization, though both contribute to flavor.

**Oven spring** — The rapid rise of bread during the first 10-15 minutes of baking, caused by expanding gases and steam. Good shaping, proper fermentation, and a hot oven with steam are the keys to dramatic oven spring.

**Peak** — The moment when your starter (or dough) has reached maximum rise. For starters, this is the ideal time to mix your dough. For bread dough, going past peak leads to over-fermentation.

**Poolish** — A type of pre-ferment made with equal parts flour and water and a small amount of commercial yeast. While not a sourdough term strictly, you may encounter it in hybrid recipes that combine sourdough and yeasted methods.

**Pre-ferment** — Any fermented mixture (starter, levain, poolish) added to dough to contribute flavor, strength, and leavening. Your sourdough starter is a natural pre-ferment.

**Pre-shape** — The first, gentle shaping of the dough after bulk fermentation. You are creating a rough shape (round or oblong) and building initial surface tension before the bench rest and final shaping.

**Proof / Proofing** — The final rise of shaped dough before baking. This can happen at room temperature (1-3 hours) or in the refrigerator overnight (cold proof / cold retard). The dough is ready when it springs back slowly when gently poked.

**Retard** — Slowing down fermentation by placing dough in the refrigerator. Cold retarding develops more complex, tangy flavors and lets you bake on your own schedule. Most bakers retard overnight (8-16 hours).

**Scoring** — Cutting the surface of proofed dough just before baking. Scoring controls where steam escapes, preventing the loaf from bursting randomly. It also allows for beautiful decorative patterns.

**Seam** — The bottom of a shaped loaf where the dough edges come together. When proofing seam-side up in a banneton, the seam becomes the top of the loaf and can split open naturally during baking for a rustic look.

**Shaping** — The process of forming your fermented dough into its final shape (boule, batard, etc.) while building surface tension. Good shaping is the difference between a loaf that holds its shape and one that spreads flat.

**Slap and fold** — A vigorous mixing technique where you lift the dough, slap it onto the counter, and fold it over itself. Excellent for building gluten in wet, sticky doughs early in the process.

**Sourdough** — Bread leavened entirely by wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria, without commercial yeast. Also used informally to refer to the starter culture itself. The oldest form of leavened bread, dating back thousands of years.

**Starter** — A living culture of wild yeast and bacteria, maintained by regular feedings of flour and water. It is the heart of all sourdough baking — your personal colony of microorganisms that leaven and flavor your bread.

**Stretch and fold** — A gentle technique for building dough strength: grab one side of the dough, stretch it upward, and fold it over to the opposite side. Rotate the bowl 90 degrees and repeat. Typically done 3-4 times during bulk fermentation.

**Surface tension** — The tautness of the outer skin of a shaped loaf. Proper surface tension helps the dough hold its shape during proofing and gives better oven spring. You create it through shaping technique.

**Tang** — The sour flavor characteristic of sourdough bread, produced by lactic and acetic acids during fermentation. Longer, cooler fermentation (like cold retarding) produces a more pronounced tang.

**Windowpane test** — A test for gluten development: stretch a small piece of dough thin enough to see light through it without it tearing. If it passes, your gluten is well-developed and ready.

**Wild yeast** — Naturally occurring yeast (primarily *Saccharomyces* species) present in flour, on fruit skins, and in the air. These are the organisms that make your starter rise — no commercial yeast packet needed.
