# The Science of Fermentation

There is a moment, usually around day five or six, when your starter stops being a science experiment and starts feeling alive. The jar on your counter is no longer just flour and water. It breathes. It rises and falls with a rhythm you did not program into it. It responds to warmth, to the flour you choose, to the very air in your kitchen. That feeling is not sentimental projection. It is the recognition of something genuinely remarkable: you are witnessing one of the oldest biological partnerships on Earth, playing out in a glass jar on your countertop.

Fermentation is not a single event. It is a conversation between organisms -- a negotiation between wild yeast and bacteria that has been going on for millennia, long before humans figured out how to harness it for bread. Understanding this conversation will not just make you a better baker. It will change how you see that bubbling jar. You will stop wondering *if* it is working and start understanding *how* it works, which means you will know exactly what to do when something goes sideways.

This chapter pulls back the curtain on that invisible world. We will walk through the organisms that call your starter home, the metabolic cycles that drive fermentation, and the elegant logic behind practices like discarding that might seem wasteful until you understand the biology.

---

## The Fermentation Cycle

Every feeding you give your starter kicks off a predictable sequence that repeats with beautiful regularity. Think of it as a four-act play.

**Act One: The Feast.** You add fresh flour and water. The microbes, which have been languishing in a depleted environment, suddenly find themselves surrounded by fresh starch and sugars. Enzymes in the flour -- amylases -- begin breaking starch molecules into simpler sugars: maltose, glucose, fructose. The table is set.

**Act Two: Exponential Growth.** The yeast and bacteria begin consuming those sugars with enthusiasm. The yeast produce carbon dioxide and ethanol. The bacteria produce lactic acid and, depending on the species, acetic acid and additional carbon dioxide. Your starter begins to rise as gas bubbles get trapped in the developing gluten network. The population of microorganisms doubles, then doubles again. Activity accelerates.

**Act Three: The Peak.** Your starter reaches its maximum height. The gas production is at its fastest, the microbial population is at its densest, and the pH has dropped into the acidic range -- typically between 3.5 and 4.2. This is the moment of peak activity, and it is when you want to use your starter for baking. The organisms are at their most vigorous, and the balance between yeast activity and bacterial acidity is optimal.

**Act Four: The Decline.** The sugars run out. The environment becomes increasingly acidic, which inhibits further microbial growth. The gluten network, weakened by acidity and enzymatic activity, can no longer hold the gas. Your starter collapses, falls back down, and a layer of liquid -- hooch -- may appear on top. The organisms enter a dormant or semi-dormant state, conserving energy until the next feeding.

This cycle typically takes eight to twelve hours at room temperature, but it can run faster in warm conditions or slower in cool ones. Once you learn to read where your starter is in this cycle, you unlock a level of baking control that recipes alone cannot give you.

## Why We Discard

The discard step confuses and frustrates nearly every beginner. You just fed this thing, and now you are supposed to throw half of it away? It feels wasteful. It feels wrong. But it is one of the most important things you do for your starter's health.

Here is the logic. Every time you feed, you are trying to give the remaining microbes enough food to thrive. If you feed without discarding, the population of organisms grows but the food-per-organism ratio shrinks. Within a few feedings, you have a massive, hungry population competing for diminishing resources. The pH drops too fast, the acid builds up before the yeast can do their work, and your starter becomes sluggish, overly sour, and weak.

Discarding resets the ratio. By removing a portion of the old culture before feeding, you ensure that the remaining organisms have ample food to consume during the next cycle. A healthy feeding ratio -- say, one part starter to five parts flour to five parts water -- means the small surviving population has plenty of fresh nutrients, enough time to go through the full fermentation cycle, and enough buffer before the acid levels become inhibitory.

> **Think of it this way:** Discarding is not throwing away your starter. It is giving the remaining culture room to breathe, eat, and thrive. The discard itself is still full of flavor and can be used in pancakes, crackers, waffles, and dozens of other recipes.

## Ecosystem Self-Regulation

One of the most elegant aspects of a sourdough starter is that it is self-regulating. You do not need to inoculate it with specific strains or monitor bacterial counts under a microscope. The system balances itself.

The acid produced by lactic acid bacteria creates an environment that is hostile to most spoilage organisms -- molds, pathogenic bacteria, and unwanted yeasts cannot survive below a pH of about 4.0. Meanwhile, the wild yeast species that thrive in sourdough have evolved to tolerate exactly this level of acidity. The bacteria, in turn, benefit from the yeast: certain yeast species break down maltose and release glucose as a byproduct, which the bacteria consume preferentially.

This mutual dependence -- yeast feeding bacteria, bacteria protecting yeast -- is why a mature starter is remarkably stable. Once established, the core microbial community resists invasion. New organisms introduced through flour or air are quickly outcompeted by the entrenched residents. Your starter becomes, in a real sense, a self-sustaining ecosystem with its own internal logic.

> **Deep Dive: Meet Your Microbes**
>
> A mature sourdough starter is not a monoculture. It is a consortium of organisms, each playing a distinct role in the ecosystem. While every starter is unique -- shaped by its flour, its environment, and its feeding history -- the same handful of species turn up again and again in starters from San Francisco to Berlin to Tokyo. Here are the principal characters.
>
> **Kazachstania humilis (Wild Yeast)**
> Formerly classified as *Candida humilis*, this is the most common yeast species in sourdough starters worldwide. Unlike commercial baker's yeast (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*), *K. humilis* thrives in the acidic environment that lactic acid bacteria create. It cannot metabolize maltose -- the dominant sugar in flour -- which turns out to be a critical feature of the symbiosis. It feeds on glucose and fructose instead, leaving the maltose for its bacterial partners. It produces carbon dioxide (the gas that makes your bread rise) and small amounts of ethanol. Its optimal temperature range is 25-30 degrees Celsius. Think of it as your starter's architect -- it builds the structure.
>
> **Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis (The Signature Bacterium)**
> This is the iconic sourdough organism, first isolated from San Francisco sourdough cultures in the 1970s and since found in starters across the globe. It is a heterofermentative lactic acid bacterium, meaning it produces not just lactic acid but also acetic acid, ethanol, and carbon dioxide. It has a unique metabolic trick: it can break down maltose, using half for energy and releasing the other half as glucose into the environment -- glucose that *K. humilis* then consumes. This cross-feeding is the metabolic handshake at the heart of sourdough symbiosis. Optimal range: 25-35 degrees Celsius. It is responsible for much of sourdough's characteristic tangy flavor.
>
> **Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (The Generalist)**
> A homofermentative bacterium, meaning it produces primarily lactic acid without significant byproducts of carbon dioxide or acetic acid. *L. plantarum* is one of the most versatile lactic acid bacteria in nature, found in everything from kimchi to salami to sourdough. It tends to dominate in starters maintained at higher temperatures or with less frequent feedings. Its lactic acid production contributes a smooth, mild sourness rather than the sharp tang of acetic acid. Optimal range: 30-35 degrees Celsius. Think of it as the stabilizer -- it keeps the pH consistently low and the environment protected.
>
> **Levilactobacillus brevis (The Flavor Artist)**
> Another heterofermentative species, *L. brevis* produces lactic acid, acetic acid, and carbon dioxide. It tends to appear in starters that are fed less frequently or maintained at cooler temperatures, where longer fermentation times favor organisms that produce complex byproducts. The acetic acid it generates is responsible for sharper, more vinegary sour notes -- the kind of flavor profile you find in a classic San Francisco sourdough. Optimal range: 20-30 degrees Celsius. It thrives when fermentation is slow and extended, which is why cold retarding your dough intensifies sour flavor.
>
> **Your Starter's Cast of Characters**
>
> | Organism | Type | Produces | Optimal Temp | Role |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | *Kazachstania humilis* | Wild yeast | CO2, ethanol | 25-30 °C | Leavening; eats glucose, leaves maltose for bacteria |
> | *F. sanfranciscensis* | Hetero LAB | Lactic acid, acetic acid, CO2 | 25-35 °C | Flavor + cross-feeds glucose to yeast |
> | *L. plantarum* | Homo LAB | Lactic acid | 30-35 °C | pH stability, mild sourness, ecosystem defense |
> | *L. brevis* | Hetero LAB | Lactic acid, acetic acid, CO2 | 20-30 °C | Complex sour flavor, thrives in slow ferments |
>
> The beauty of this system is its redundancy and resilience. If one population dips -- say, the yeast slow down during a cold snap -- the bacteria continue producing acid that keeps the environment safe until conditions improve. If you switch flours, the relative populations may shift, but the core partnership between yeast and bacteria persists. Your starter is not fragile. It is an ecosystem that has been refining itself for thousands of years, and it carries that resilience into your kitchen.

## Pro Tips

- **Watch, do not clock.** Timing your starter by hours alone will mislead you. A starter that peaks in six hours at 28 degrees will take twelve at 20 degrees. Learn to read the visual cues -- the dome, the bubbles, the texture -- and you will never be caught off guard by a change in season or kitchen temperature.

- **Taste your starter at different stages.** At peak, it should taste pleasantly tangy with a yeasty sweetness. If it tastes sharply vinegary or like nail polish remover, it has gone too long without feeding. Tasting is the fastest diagnostic tool you have.

- **The float test has limits.** A spoonful of starter floating in water indicates gas production, but not necessarily peak activity. A very young starter can produce gas without having a stable microbial community. Use the float test as one data point, not the only one.

- **Temperature is your most powerful lever.** If your starter is sluggish, move it somewhere warmer. If it is overactive and peaking before you can use it, feed it with cooler water or increase the feeding ratio. You are not at the mercy of your starter -- you are its climate controller.

- **Every starter is local.** Your starter will develop a microbial fingerprint shaped by your flour, your water, your kitchen, and your schedule. Two starters fed identically in different cities will taste different. This is not a flaw. It is terroir, and it is part of what makes sourdough baking personal.
