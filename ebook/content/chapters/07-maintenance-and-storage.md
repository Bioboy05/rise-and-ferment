# Maintenance & Storage

A sourdough starter is not a pet, but it is not far off. It needs feeding, it responds to its environment, and it rewards consistency with reliable performance. The good news is that unlike a pet, a starter is remarkably flexible about how much attention you give it. You can feed it daily, weekly, or park it in the back of your freezer for months. The right maintenance schedule depends entirely on how often you bake.

This chapter covers the full spectrum, from the daily rhythms of an active baker to the long hibernation strategies for someone who only bakes a few times a year. It also addresses the question that haunts every sourdough beginner: what do I do with my starter when I go on vacation?

The answer, as you will see, is that your starter is far more resilient than you think. It has survived in human kitchens for millennia, through famines and floods and centuries of neglect. It can certainly survive your two-week trip to the coast.

---

## Active Schedule: Daily Feeding

If you bake frequently -- three or more times per week -- keeping your starter at room temperature on a daily feeding schedule is the most practical approach. A daily-fed starter is always ready to go. You do not need to plan ahead or spend two days reviving it before a bake.

A typical daily routine looks like this: each morning, discard all but 20-30 grams of your starter, then feed it with fresh flour and water at a ratio of roughly 1:5:5 (one part starter, five parts flour, five parts water by weight). By evening, it will have peaked and begun its descent. If you need it for baking, catch it at peak -- usually six to ten hours after feeding, depending on your kitchen temperature.

The discard from each feeding can go straight into pancake batter, crackers, waffles, or any of the dozens of discard recipes that make sourdough baking a zero-waste practice.

> **Consistency matters more than precision.** Feed at roughly the same time each day with roughly the same ratio. Your starter's microbial community thrives on predictability. Erratic schedules produce erratic results.

## Low-Maintenance: The Fridge Method

Most home bakers do not bake every day, and that is perfectly fine. The refrigerator is your starter's best friend. Cold temperatures slow fermentation to a crawl, meaning your starter can go a week or more between feedings without any harm.

Here is the routine. Feed your starter as normal. Let it rise at room temperature for one to two hours -- just long enough that the microbes are active and have begun fermenting. Then cap the jar (not airtight; leave it slightly loose or poke a small hole in the lid) and place it in the refrigerator. It will continue fermenting very slowly in the cold, and it will be perfectly healthy for seven to ten days.

When you are ready to bake, take the starter out of the fridge and give it one or two room-temperature feedings to wake it up. The first feeding might be sluggish -- the starter may take twelve hours or more to peak. The second feeding should show much stronger activity, typically doubling in six to eight hours. Once it is rising and falling predictably, it is ready for your dough.

If you do not bake for a while, simply pull the jar out once a week, discard, feed, let it sit for an hour or two, and return it to the fridge. This minimal weekly attention is enough to keep a starter healthy indefinitely.

## Vacation Mode: Two to Four Weeks

Going away for a couple of weeks? Your starter will be fine. Give it a generous feeding before you leave -- a stiff feed at a ratio of 1:10:10 or even higher -- and refrigerate it. The large quantity of fresh flour gives the microbes a vast food supply to work through slowly in the cold. Two to three weeks in the fridge under these conditions is entirely routine.

When you return, the starter may look unappetizing. There might be a dark layer of hooch (liquid) on top, a greyish color, and a strong, alcoholic smell. This is normal. Pour off the hooch, discard all but a tablespoon, and begin feeding. It may take three or four feedings over two days to return to full vigor, but it will come back. Starters are survivors.

For trips longer than a month, consider drying some of your starter as a backup (see below). But honestly, starters have been revived after months in the fridge. The organisms do not die easily.

## Long-Term Storage: Drying Your Starter

Drying is the ultimate insurance policy. A dried starter can be stored at room temperature for years and revived with nothing more than flour and water.

The process is simple. Spread a thin layer of active starter onto a sheet of parchment paper. Let it dry completely at room temperature -- this takes one to two days depending on humidity. Once it is fully dry, it will be brittle and flaky. Break it into small pieces, place them in an airtight jar or zip-lock bag, and store in a cool, dry place. Label it with the date.

To revive, dissolve a tablespoon of dried flakes in a small amount of warm water (about 30 grams), stir in 30 grams of flour, and begin feeding as if you are creating a new starter. It typically takes three to five days of regular feeding for the dried culture to rehydrate and return to full activity. The microbial community may shift slightly during the drying and revival process, but the core organisms will survive.

> **Keep a backup.** Even if you maintain your starter in the fridge and bake weekly, consider drying a small amount once or twice a year. Store it somewhere separate from your main jar. If anything ever goes wrong -- contamination, a forgotten feeding, a fridge malfunction -- you can start fresh from your backup in less than a week.

> **Deep Dive: The Seasonal Starter Calendar**
>
> Your starter does not exist in a vacuum. It responds to the world around it, and the single biggest environmental factor it faces is temperature. As the seasons change, your kitchen temperature shifts, and your starter's behavior shifts with it. Understanding these seasonal patterns turns frustration into anticipation. Here is what to expect through the year.
>
> **Spring (March - May): The Awakening**
>
> If you have been baking through winter with a sluggish starter, spring feels like a revelation. As kitchen temperatures climb from 18-19 degrees into the low twenties, your starter wakes up. Fermentation accelerates. Peaks that took fourteen hours in January now arrive in ten. This is the time to pay attention to your timing, because the schedule that worked all winter will start running ahead of you.
>
> Feeding ratio: 1:5:5 works well as temperatures rise. If your starter starts peaking faster than you expect, bump to 1:6:6 or 1:7:7. Water temperature: use room temperature water (22-25 degrees Celsius), no need for warm water anymore. Common spring problem: your starter peaks while you are asleep or at work because you have not adjusted for the faster pace. Solution: feed later in the evening, or increase the ratio to slow things down.
>
> **Summer (June - August): The Sprint**
>
> Summer is when starters go into overdrive. Kitchen temperatures above 25 degrees -- and often above 28 or 30 in homes without air conditioning -- push fermentation into high gear. Your starter might peak in four to six hours instead of eight to twelve. The acetic acid bacteria become less active relative to the lactic acid bacteria, which means less tang and more mild sourness.
>
> Feeding ratio: increase to 1:8:8 or even 1:10:10 to give the microbes more food and slow the cycle. In extreme heat, you may need to feed twice daily. Water temperature: use cool water, 18-20 degrees Celsius, straight from the tap or even briefly refrigerated. Common summer problems: over-fermentation (starter collapses before you can use it), excessive hooch production, thin consistency, and an alcohol-forward smell. Solution: higher ratios, cooler water, and consider moving to the fridge between feeds even if you are baking daily. The fridge is not just for storage -- it is a temperature management tool.
>
> **Autumn (September - November): The Golden Season**
>
> Many experienced bakers consider autumn the best time of year for sourdough. Kitchen temperatures settle into the ideal range of 21-25 degrees. Fermentation is predictable. The starter peaks at a reasonable pace. The balance between yeast and bacteria tends to be optimal, producing bread with both good rise and complex flavor.
>
> Feeding ratio: 1:5:5 is the sweet spot for most kitchens. Water temperature: room temperature (22-25 degrees). This is an excellent time to experiment with new flours, longer fermentation schedules, or recipes that require precise timing. Your starter is at its most reliable, so use that consistency to push your skills. Common autumn adjustment: as temperatures begin to drop in late November, start watching for the slowdown that signals winter is approaching.
>
> **Winter (December - February): The Long Game**
>
> Cold kitchens mean slow fermentation. If your kitchen drops below 20 degrees -- and in many homes it dips to 16-18 degrees overnight -- your starter may take sixteen to twenty hours to peak, or it may not peak fully at all. This is not a sign of an unhealthy starter. It is physics. The organisms are still alive; they are simply working more slowly.
>
> Feeding ratio: drop to 1:3:3 or even 1:2:2 to ensure there are enough organisms relative to the available food. A lower ratio means the culture reaches critical mass faster. Water temperature: use warm water, 28-32 degrees Celsius, to give the starter a thermal boost at feeding time. Common winter problems: starter seems dead or barely active, takes forever to rise, bread is dense. Solutions: find a warm spot in your kitchen -- on top of the refrigerator, near the oven (not on it), inside the oven with just the light on (this creates a steady 27-30 degree environment). A seedling heat mat set to 26-28 degrees is one of the best investments a cold-climate baker can make.
>
> **Quick Seasonal Reference**
>
> | Season | Kitchen Temp | Ratio | Water Temp | Peak Time | Key Adjustment |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Spring | 20-24 °C | 1:5:5 to 1:7:7 | 22-25 °C | 8-10 h | Watch for faster peaks; increase ratio if needed |
> | Summer | 25-32 °C | 1:8:8 to 1:10:10 | 18-20 °C | 4-6 h | Cool water, higher ratios, fridge between feeds |
> | Autumn | 21-25 °C | 1:5:5 | 22-25 °C | 8-12 h | Ideal conditions; experiment and refine technique |
> | Winter | 16-20 °C | 1:2:2 to 1:3:3 | 28-32 °C | 12-20 h | Warm water, warm spot, lower ratios |
>
> The most important takeaway: there is no single "correct" feeding schedule. The correct schedule is the one that accounts for your current kitchen temperature and produces a starter that peaks predictably within a window you can work with. Seasons change, and your approach should change with them.

## Pro Tips

- **The oven light trick.** If your kitchen is cold, place your starter in the oven with only the light turned on. Most oven lights generate enough warmth to maintain 27-30 degrees Celsius -- the ideal range for active fermentation. Just do not forget it is in there before you preheat for baking.

- **Keep the jar small.** Use a jar that is only two to three times the volume of your starter after feeding. A jar that is too large makes it difficult to judge how much the starter has risen. A tall, narrow jar is ideal because it makes volume changes easy to track. A rubber band around the jar at the post-feeding level gives you a clear reference point.

- **Date your fridge starter.** Stick a small piece of tape on the jar with the date of the last feeding. When life gets busy, it is easy to lose track of whether it has been five days or twelve. The tape removes all guesswork.

- **Two jars are better than one.** Maintain a small backup jar in the fridge, fed once every two weeks, separate from your active baking starter. If your main culture ever gets contaminated, forgotten, or accidentally tossed by a well-meaning housemate, you have insurance.

- **Flour matters for storage.** When feeding a starter that will go into the fridge for an extended period, use a higher-protein bread flour rather than whole wheat or rye. Whole grain flours ferment faster due to their higher enzyme activity, which means the culture will exhaust its food supply sooner in the fridge. Bread flour gives it a slower, steadier fuel source for the long wait.
