# Your First Bread

There is a particular kind of nervousness that comes right before you bake your first sourdough loaf. You have spent days -- maybe weeks -- feeding your starter, watching it rise and fall, learning its rhythms. Now you are about to find out whether all of that patience actually leads somewhere. Whether the bubbling jar on your counter can do what ten thousand years of human bakers say it can: make bread.

Let me tell you something that will save you a great deal of anxiety: your first loaf will not be perfect. It might be flat. It might be dense. The scoring might tear in an unexpected direction. The crumb might be tighter than the Instagram photos you have been studying. And none of that matters. What matters is that you made bread from flour, water, salt, and a culture you raised yourself. That is extraordinary, and it deserves to be celebrated regardless of how the crumb looks.

This chapter walks you through a simple, forgiving recipe designed specifically for first-time sourdough bakers. We will cover every step in detail, explain the why behind each technique, and give you the confidence to slide that dough into the oven knowing exactly what to expect.

---

## The Recipe

This is a straightforward white sourdough loaf. It uses a moderate hydration that is easy to handle, a small amount of ingredients, and a timeline you can fit into a single day with an overnight cold proof.

**Ingredients:**

- 100 g active sourdough starter (at peak, passed float test)
- 400 g strong bread flour (11.5-13% protein)
- 280 g water (room temperature, around 25 degrees Celsius)
- 8 g fine sea salt

**Equipment:** Large mixing bowl, bench scraper, kitchen scale, banneton or bowl lined with a floured tea towel, Dutch oven with lid, lame or sharp razor blade, parchment paper.

## Step by Step

**Autolyse (45 minutes).** In your mixing bowl, combine the flour and water. Mix with your hand until no dry flour remains -- it will look shaggy and rough, and that is exactly right. Cover the bowl and let it rest for 45 minutes. During this rest, the flour fully hydrates and enzymes begin breaking down starches and developing gluten. When you come back, the dough will feel noticeably smoother and more extensible. This single step makes every subsequent step easier.

**Add starter and salt.** Spread your active starter over the top of the dough. Sprinkle the salt on top. Now work them in using the pinch-and-fold method: pinch the dough between your thumb and fingers, cutting through it, then fold it over on itself. Rotate the bowl and repeat. Continue for two to three minutes until the starter and salt are fully incorporated and the dough feels cohesive.

**Stretch and fold (2 hours, 4 sets).** Over the next two hours, you will perform four sets of stretch and folds, spaced thirty minutes apart. For each set, wet your hand, slide it under one side of the dough, stretch it upward until you feel resistance, and fold it over the center of the dough. Rotate the bowl ninety degrees and repeat. Four stretches per set, four sets total. You will feel the dough transform under your hands -- from slack and sticky in the first set to smooth, elastic, and billowy by the fourth. This is gluten development happening in real time.

**Bulk fermentation (3-5 hours).** After your final stretch and fold, cover the bowl and leave the dough at room temperature. This is the longest and most important phase. The yeast and bacteria in your starter are now working through the dough, producing gas, developing flavor, and building structure. The dough is ready when it has increased in volume by roughly fifty percent, feels airy and jiggly when you nudge the bowl, and you can see bubbles on the surface and around the edges. In a warm kitchen (25-27 degrees), this might take three hours. In a cooler one (20-22 degrees), it could take five. Watch the dough, not the clock.

> **How to tell when bulk is done:** Gently poke the dough with a floured finger. It should spring back slowly and leave a slight indentation. If it springs back instantly, it needs more time. If it does not spring back at all, you have likely gone too far -- but do not panic. Shape it and proceed.

**Pre-shape.** Flour your work surface lightly. Turn the dough out and, using your bench scraper, gently shape it into a rough round by tucking the edges underneath. Let it rest, uncovered, for twenty minutes. This brief rest relaxes the gluten and makes final shaping easier.

**Final shape.** Flour the top of the dough lightly, then flip it so the floured side is down. Pull the bottom edge up to the center, then the left side over, then the right side over, then roll the top edge down and toward you, creating a tight package. Flip it seam-side up and place it into your floured banneton or lined bowl. The seam side faces up now because it will become the bottom of the loaf when you turn it out for baking.

**Cold proof (8-14 hours).** Cover the banneton with a plastic bag or damp towel and place it in the refrigerator. Overnight is ideal. The cold slows fermentation dramatically, giving you scheduling flexibility and developing deeper, more complex flavors. You can bake it after eight hours, or it can wait up to fourteen.

**Bake.** Place your Dutch oven (lid on) in the oven and preheat to 250 degrees Celsius for at least forty-five minutes. Yes, forty-five minutes -- the Dutch oven needs to be screaming hot. Cut a piece of parchment paper, take the dough from the fridge, invert the banneton onto the parchment, and score the top with a decisive slash about one centimeter deep at a thirty-degree angle. Lift the parchment into the hot Dutch oven, cover with the lid, and bake for 20 minutes. Then remove the lid, reduce the temperature to 230 degrees, and bake for another 20-25 minutes until the crust is deeply golden brown. The internal temperature should reach 95-98 degrees Celsius.

**Cool.** Transfer the loaf to a wire rack immediately. Now comes the hardest part of the entire process: wait at least one hour before cutting. The bread is still cooking internally as it cools, and the crumb structure is setting. Cutting too early releases steam, makes the interior gummy, and undermines all the work you just did. Listen for the crackle of the crust as it cools -- bakers call this "singing," and it is one of the most satisfying sounds in the kitchen.

> **Deep Dive: 10 Most Common First-Loaf Mistakes**
>
> Almost every beginner makes at least two or three of these. Knowing them in advance will not prevent all of them -- some lessons only stick when you experience them firsthand -- but this list will help you diagnose what happened and fix it on your next bake.
>
> **1. Using starter that is not mature enough.** Your starter needs to be genuinely active -- rising predictably, doubling in volume within four to eight hours, passing the float test. A starter that is only a week old may produce bubbles but lack the microbial strength to leaven a full loaf. The result is a dense, heavy bread that barely rose. Fix: be patient. Most starters need two to four weeks of consistent feeding before they are ready to bake with.
>
> **2. Skipping the autolyse.** It seems like a pointless wait, but autolyse transforms your dough. Without it, you will fight a sticky, shaggy mess during stretch and folds, and the gluten will be noticeably weaker. Fix: always autolyse. Even thirty minutes makes a significant difference. Your future self will thank you.
>
> **3. Under-fermenting the bulk.** This is the single most common mistake. The dough looks the same as it did two hours ago, but you are impatient, so you shape it anyway. Under-fermented bread is dense, gummy in the center, has a tight crumb with no open holes, and tastes bland. Fix: wait. Look for a fifty-percent volume increase, visible bubbles, and a jiggly, airy texture. If you are unsure, give it another thirty minutes.
>
> **4. Over-fermenting the bulk.** The opposite problem. You left the dough too long, the yeast exhausted the available sugars, and the gluten broke down from excess acidity. The dough feels slack, sticky, and lifeless. It spreads rather than holding its shape. The bread comes out flat with a gummy, collapsed crumb. Fix: in warm kitchens, check your dough more frequently. Learn what the "just right" stage looks and feels like. Reduce room temperature or use cooler water if your kitchen is consistently above 26 degrees.
>
> **5. Weak shaping.** Shaping creates the surface tension that gives your loaf structure and height. If you handle the dough timidly, the loaf will spread sideways in the oven instead of springing upward. Fix: be firm but gentle. Pull the dough toward you on the counter to create tension on the surface. The bottom seam should feel taut. Practice improves this dramatically.
>
> **6. Not preheating the Dutch oven long enough.** Fifteen or twenty minutes is not enough. The cast iron needs to be uniformly heated to its core, not just hot on the outside. Insufficient preheating means less oven spring and a paler, softer crust. Fix: forty-five minutes minimum at 250 degrees Celsius. Set a timer. Do not cheat.
>
> **7. Scoring too shallow.** A tentative score -- barely scratching the surface -- does not give the bread a controlled place to expand. The loaf will tear open in unpredictable ways, often along the sides or bottom. Fix: hold your blade at a thirty-degree angle and cut decisively to a depth of about one centimeter. One confident slash is better than several timid ones.
>
> **8. Opening the oven door during the first twenty minutes.** Steam is essential for oven spring. The lid of your Dutch oven traps steam from the dough itself, keeping the crust pliable while the interior expands. Opening the lid releases that steam and kills the spring. Fix: trust the process. Do not open the lid for any reason during the first twenty minutes. Not to check, not to peek, not to adjust.
>
> **9. Cutting the bread before it has cooled.** This is agony, because the bread smells incredible and you want to see the crumb immediately. But the interior is still above 90 degrees and the starches have not set. Cutting now means a gummy, wet interior that you will wrongly blame on under-baking. Fix: wait a full hour. Go for a walk. Clean the kitchen. Let the bread finish its work.
>
> **10. Using the wrong flour.** All-purpose flour, with its lower protein content, will give you a flatter loaf with less oven spring and a tighter crumb. Whole wheat absorbs more water and ferments faster, which catches beginners off guard. Fix: for your first loaf, use strong bread flour (11.5 to 13 percent protein). Save the whole wheat blends and specialty flours for once you have the basic process dialed in.
>
> The good news: every single one of these mistakes is fixable on the next bake. Sourdough is forgiving. Even a "failed" loaf usually tastes great toasted with butter. The only real failure is not baking a second one.

## Your Imperfect Loaf Is Still a Triumph

Read this part carefully, because it is more important than any technique in this chapter.

Your first loaf is almost certainly going to be imperfect. The crust might not have that glassy shatter you have seen in photographs. The crumb might be dense in the center, or the scoring might have gone crooked, or the bottom might be a shade darker than you wanted. You might look at it and feel a flicker of disappointment.

Resist that feeling. What you have in your hands is a loaf of bread made from three ingredients and a jar of wild microorganisms that you cultivated yourself. No commercial yeast. No preservatives. No factory. Just flour, water, salt, and time. People have been making bread exactly this way for thousands of years, and you just joined their ranks.

Slice it. Toast it. Spread butter on it. Share it with someone. And then bake another one. The second loaf will be better. The third will be better still. That is the nature of craft -- it rewards persistence, not perfection.

## Pro Tips

- **Keep a baking journal.** Write down your timeline, kitchen temperature, flour brand, hydration, and how the dough felt at each stage. Take a photo of the crumb. Within five or six bakes, patterns will emerge that no recipe can teach you.

- **Preheat longer than you think.** If your crust is pale and soft, the most likely culprit is insufficient preheating. A full hour at maximum temperature is not excessive for heavy cast iron.

- **Cold dough scores better.** Scoring dough straight from the refrigerator is dramatically easier than scoring room-temperature dough. The cold firms up the surface, letting the blade glide cleanly. This is one of the best reasons to use an overnight cold proof.

- **Do not flour the scoring blade.** Oil it lightly instead, or dip it in water. Flour clogs the blade and creates drag, which leads to tearing instead of clean cuts.

- **Taste before you judge.** A loaf that looks unremarkable can taste extraordinary. Sourdough flavor develops over hours after baking -- the loaf you cut into at hour one will taste different (and often better) at hour four. Give it time.
