# Trending Techniques

The sourdough world does not stand still. Social media has accelerated innovation at a staggering pace, with bakers on opposite sides of the planet sharing techniques that once took generations to travel between villages. A home baker in Tokyo discovers a method on a Tuesday, films it, and by Thursday it is being replicated in kitchens across Buenos Aires, Oslo, and Cape Town. This chapter covers the most exciting developments — clever shortcuts, creative extensions, and ideas that push sourdough into new territory. Start with the one that excites you most and give it three honest attempts before judging the results.

---

## Croissant Sourdough Bread

This is the technique that broke the internet — and for good reason. Traditional croissant lamination requires hours of folding, chilling, and rolling butter into dough with geometric precision. Croissant sourdough bread bypasses all of that with one beautifully simple idea: grate frozen butter directly into the dough during shaping.

Take a block of unsalted butter — 100 to 150 grams for a standard loaf — and freeze it solid for at least two hours. When you reach the shaping stage, turn the dough out and stretch it into a rough rectangle. Using the coarse side of a box grater, shred the frozen butter over the surface in an even layer. Fold the dough in thirds like a letter, rotate ninety degrees, and stretch it out again. Repeat the fold one more time, then shape into a boule or batard as usual.

The frozen butter creates distinct pockets throughout the crumb — not the hundreds of paper-thin layers you get in a true croissant, but generous, flaky striations that shatter when you tear into them. The crust bakes to a deep, bronzed gold from the butter rendering at high heat.

Speed is critical. If the butter softens and melts into the dough, you lose the layering effect entirely. Keep a cold bench scraper handy and return the shaped loaf to the freezer for ten minutes if the butter starts to feel greasy. Cold proof overnight, then bake in a Dutch oven at your standard temperature.

## Bread Art & Stenciling

Chapter 12 covered scoring as a functional and artistic tool. Bread art takes that canvas further — adding color, texture, and an entirely new vocabulary of visual techniques.

**Natural stencils** are the simplest entry point. Place fresh herb sprigs, small leaves, or laser-cut rice paper shapes onto the surface of cold, shaped dough. Dust flour over the top, then carefully lift the stencils away. The covered areas remain dark while the exposed dough bakes white — a photographic negative effect that produces stunning botanical silhouettes.

**Colored flour** opens another dimension. Turmeric mixed into rice flour creates vivid gold, beetroot powder produces deep red, activated charcoal gives true black, and matcha yields soft green. Dust these through paper stencils or sift freehand for abstract designs. The trick is restraint — a single accent color on a white-flour background is far more striking than a rainbow.

**Bread scissors** (lames ciseaux) allow you to snip the dough surface into small peaked cuts that lift and crisp independently during baking — a three-dimensional texture that no blade can replicate.

The golden rule of bread art is to work on cold dough, straight from the refrigerator. Warm dough sticks to stencils, smears color, and resists clean cuts. Plan your design on paper first and keep it simple for your initial attempts.

## Sourdough Donuts

If you have made enriched sourdough — the brioche-style dough from Recipe 5 — you are already ninety percent of the way to extraordinary donuts.

Start with an enriched dough containing more sugar (60-80 grams per 500 grams of flour) and butter (80-100 grams). After bulk fermentation, roll it out to 1.5 centimeters thick. Cut ring donuts with a cutter, or squares for filled donuts. Proof at room temperature for 60 to 90 minutes until puffy — shorter than a bread proof, because overproofed donuts absorb too much oil.

Fry in neutral oil heated to 175 degrees Celsius, roughly 60 to 90 seconds per side. Drain on a wire rack, not paper towels, so the bottoms stay crisp.

Glaze while warm — a simple sugar glaze, cinnamon sugar, or chocolate ganache all work beautifully. The sourdough tang running beneath the sweetness creates a complexity that commercial donuts cannot touch.

**A note on safety:** frying requires your full attention. Use a deep, heavy pot filled no more than halfway with oil, keep a lid nearby to smother any flare-up, and use a thermometer to maintain a steady temperature.

## Flavored Starters

Your main starter should stay on its strict diet of flour and water — consistency is how you build reliability. But nothing stops you from maintaining small satellite starters that eat something more interesting.

A **grape juice starter** replaces the water in each feeding with unsweetened grape juice. The natural sugars and wild yeasts on grape skins produce a starter with fruity, almost wine-like undertones that makes remarkable pizza dough.

A **honey-fed starter** adds one tablespoon of raw honey per feeding. The result is subtly sweet, with a mellow acidity that works well in enriched breads and sandwich loaves.

A **rye-dominant starter** — fed exclusively on dark rye flour — develops an earthy, deeply malty character that transforms pumpernickel and rye breads. It ferments aggressively, so feed it at a higher ratio (1:5:5 minimum) and keep it cool.

**Fruit-infused starters** using apple juice or pineapple juice during the initial creation phase are popular for kickstarting fermentation. The acidity discourages unwanted bacteria in the early days, giving your target organisms a head start.

Keep flavored starters in small quantities — 50 to 100 grams — and store them in the refrigerator, feeding once a week. They are for experimentation, not daily drivers.

## Sourdough Beyond Bread

The most exciting trend in sourdough is the realization that it does not belong exclusively in a loaf. Anywhere your recipe calls for flour and water, sourdough can contribute its signature tang and improved texture.

**Sourdough pasta** replaces a portion of the water in pasta dough with active starter — roughly 100 grams per 300 grams of flour. The fermentation adds chew and a faint acidity that pairs well with rich sauces.

**Sourdough bagels** are a natural fit. The classic bagel already relies on a tangy, dense crumb — sourdough amplifies that character beyond what commercial yeast alone can achieve.

**Fermented hot sauce** uses starter discard as the fermentation engine. Blend your peppers with a spoonful of discard, salt to taste, and ferment at room temperature for five to seven days.

**Sourdough chocolate cake** is the discard recipe that converts skeptics. Replace the buttermilk in your favorite recipe with an equal weight of active discard — the tang balances sweetness, and the crumb stays soft for days.

The principle is simple: if it uses flour and water, sourdough has something to offer.

## Pro Tips

- **Start with croissant sourdough bread if you want to impress.** It has the highest reward-to-effort ratio of any trending technique — the method adds only ten minutes to your normal shaping routine.

- **For bread art, practice scoring on a ball of cold Play-Doh before committing to real dough.** It has a similar resistance to cold sourdough and lets you experiment with patterns risk-free.

- **Sourdough donuts are best eaten within hours of frying.** The butter and sugar content accelerates moisture migration, so they stale faster than yeasted donuts. Fry only what you will eat that day.

- **Keep flavored starters in small quantities — 50 to 100 grams — and feed them weekly in the fridge.** If one dies from neglect, starting a new flavored starter takes three days, not the two weeks your main culture required.

- **Photograph your bread in natural light near a window.** Overhead shots for scoring, cross-section shots for crumb, and always on a simple background. The bread is the star.
