# Understanding Flour Types

Open the baking aisle in any well-stocked grocery store and you are faced with a wall of white bags, each promising something slightly different. Bread flour, all-purpose, whole wheat, rye, spelt, tipo 00, T65 — the labels blur together, and the differences feel academic until you realize that flour is not just an ingredient in sourdough. It is the ingredient. It determines how your dough handles, how your crumb develops, how your crust browns, and — more than almost anything else — how your bread tastes.

The good news is that understanding flour does not require a food science degree. It requires knowing one number (protein content), understanding one concept (extraction rate), and then being brave enough to experiment. Once you grasp those fundamentals, you will stop buying flour by brand loyalty and start buying it by specification. That shift is the difference between a baker who follows recipes and a baker who designs them.

Let us open a few bags and see what is actually inside.

---

## Protein: The Number That Matters Most

When bakers talk about flour, they talk about protein. Protein content — listed on the nutritional label as a percentage — tells you how much gluten your dough can potentially form. Gluten is the elastic network that traps gas from fermentation, giving your bread its rise and its chew. More protein means a stronger network. Less protein means a softer, more tender crumb.

**Bread flour** typically contains 11-13% protein. It is the workhorse of sourdough baking, providing enough strength to support a tall, open crumb without making the dough tough or difficult to shape. If you are new to sourdough and want one flour to start with, a good bread flour is the answer.

**All-purpose flour** sits lower at 9-11% protein. It can absolutely make sourdough bread, but the resulting loaf will be denser, with a tighter crumb and a softer crust. Many bakers use all-purpose for enriched doughs — where butter and sugar are involved — because the lower protein produces a more tender texture. For lean sourdough loaves, though, you will fight against its limitations.

**Whole wheat flour** changes the equation entirely. It contains the entire grain — bran, germ, and endosperm — which means more protein on paper (often 13-14%), but that protein behaves differently. The sharp bran particles cut through gluten strands as you knead, and the germ adds oils that can interfere with gluten development. The result is a denser, more flavorful bread that requires higher hydration and more careful handling. Start by replacing 10-20% of your bread flour with whole wheat, then increase as you gain confidence.

**Rye flour** lives by its own rules. Rye proteins do not form gluten the way wheat proteins do. Instead, rye relies on pentosans — complex sugars that absorb water and create a sticky, gel-like structure. This is why 100% rye breads are dense, moist, and almost cake-like. In blends, rye adds fermentation fuel (the sugars that wild yeast and bacteria love), earthy complexity, and a distinctive tang. Even a small percentage of rye can accelerate your starter's activity.

## Extraction and Color

When millers process wheat, they can remove varying amounts of the outer bran and germ. The percentage of the original grain that remains in the final flour is called the **extraction rate**. White bread flour has a low extraction rate — roughly 72-76% — meaning nearly a quarter of the grain has been sifted away. Whole wheat has a 100% extraction rate: nothing removed.

European flours use a naming system tied to ash content, which correlates closely with extraction. French **T65** is a creamy, slightly rustic flour with more character than pure white bread flour but less heaviness than whole wheat — think of it as the sweet spot. Italian **tipo 00** is ultra-refined, with a silky texture and moderate protein, designed for extensible doughs like pizza and focaccia.

Understanding extraction helps you predict behavior: higher extraction means more flavor and nutrition, but also more water absorption, faster fermentation (the microbes feast on the extra sugars), and a denser crumb. Lower extraction means a lighter, more predictable dough with a milder flavor.

## Mixing Flours: Where Creativity Begins

The real magic happens when you stop using a single flour and start blending. Every professional baker has a house blend — a proprietary combination tuned to their oven, their schedule, and their flavor goals. You can develop yours too, and it is simpler than it sounds.

The principle is straightforward: use a strong base flour for structure, then add smaller proportions of other flours for flavor, nutrition, and character. Your base should always be at least 50-60% of the total to ensure enough gluten strength for a proper rise. Beyond that, you are free to explore.

When blending, keep two things in mind. First, **hydration will change.** Whole grains and rye absorb significantly more water than white flour, so you will need to increase your water by 2-5% for every 10% of whole grain flour you add. Second, **fermentation will accelerate.** Whole grain and rye flours introduce more sugars and more naturally occurring microorganisms, which means your dough may ferment faster than you expect. Watch the dough, not the clock.

> ## The Flour Blend Lab
>
> Below are six tested flour blends, each designed for a specific purpose. These are not theoretical suggestions — they are formulas that produce consistent, predictable results. For each blend, the percentages refer to the total flour weight in your recipe. Hydration adjustments are relative to what you would use with 100% bread flour.
>
> **Blend 1: "The Workhorse"**
> 80% bread flour / 20% whole wheat
> *Hydration adjustment:* +3-5% over your base
> *Flavor profile:* Mildly nutty with a subtle sweetness from the wheat germ. Clean, familiar sourdough taste without any aggressive flavors.
> *Rise characteristics:* Strong, reliable rise with an open but not wild crumb. Very forgiving of timing errors.
> *Best for:* Everyday sandwich loaves, your go-to bread. This is the blend to master first.
> *Handling notes:* No special treatment required. Autolyse for 30 minutes improves texture but is optional.
>
> **Blend 2: "The Nutritionist"**
> 60% bread flour / 20% whole wheat / 20% dark rye
> *Hydration adjustment:* +8-10%
> *Flavor profile:* Earthy and complex with a gentle tang from the rye. Noticeable grain flavor without being heavy.
> *Rise characteristics:* Moderate rise. Crumb is tighter than the Workhorse but still pleasantly open. The rye brings moisture that keeps the bread fresh longer.
> *Best for:* Hearty table bread, toast bread, open-faced sandwiches.
> *Handling notes:* Dough will be stickier than all-wheat blends. Resist the temptation to add more flour — use wet hands and a bench scraper instead. Bulk fermentation may be 15-20% shorter due to the rye sugars.
>
> **Blend 3: "The Artisan"**
> 70% T65 flour / 15% whole wheat / 15% rye
> *Hydration adjustment:* +5-7%
> *Flavor profile:* Creamy, complex, with a subtle rustic character. The T65 base provides a depth that pure white flour cannot match, while the whole wheat and rye add layers without dominating.
> *Rise characteristics:* Good oven spring with an irregular, artisan-style crumb. Beautiful caramelized crust.
> *Best for:* Country-style boules, batards, any bread you want to look and taste like it came from a French bakery.
> *Handling notes:* T65 can vary between brands — look for one with 11-12% protein. Autolyse of 45-60 minutes is strongly recommended to let the T65 hydrate fully.
>
> **Blend 4: "The Scandinavian"**
> 50% bread flour / 50% dark rye
> *Hydration adjustment:* +15-20%
> *Flavor profile:* Boldly earthy, deeply tangy, with notes of molasses and dried fruit. This is not subtle bread — it is bread with something to say.
> *Rise characteristics:* Low, dense rise. Expect a tight, moist crumb similar to traditional Nordic rye breads. This is not a failure — it is the style.
> *Best for:* Danish rugbrod-style loaves, thin-sliced sandwich bread, smoked salmon open sandwiches, cheese platters.
> *Handling notes:* This blend requires a completely different approach. Use a loaf pan rather than free-form shaping — the dough will not hold its shape. Mix gently; excessive kneading develops a gummy texture. Bake at a lower temperature (200-210°C) for a longer time (60-75 minutes). Let the bread rest for at least 24 hours before slicing — rye bread needs time to set its crumb.
>
> **Blend 5: "The Mediterranean"**
> 85% tipo 00 flour / 15% semolina (durum wheat)
> *Hydration adjustment:* -2-3% (tipo 00 absorbs less water; semolina absorbs slowly)
> *Flavor profile:* Buttery, golden, with a gentle sweetness from the semolina. Light and elegant.
> *Rise characteristics:* Good rise with a tender, almost pillowy crumb. The crust is thinner and crispier than wheat-heavy blends.
> *Best for:* Focaccia, ciabatta, pizza dough, any Mediterranean-style flatbread or enriched bread.
> *Handling notes:* Tipo 00 produces a very extensible dough — it stretches easily but may not hold shape during proofing as well as bread flour. Use a banneton or couche for support. The semolina should be fine-ground (semola rimacinata), not coarse — coarse semolina creates a gritty texture.
>
> **Blend 6: "The Ancient Grains"**
> 60% bread flour / 20% whole spelt / 20% whole einkorn
> *Hydration adjustment:* +5-8%
> *Flavor profile:* Honey-like sweetness from the einkorn, a mild nuttiness from the spelt, and a complexity that modern wheat simply cannot replicate. This is the most interesting flavor blend in the collection.
> *Rise characteristics:* Moderate rise. Spelt and einkorn gluten is weaker and more fragile than modern wheat gluten, so handle gently. Expect a moderately open crumb with a golden hue from the einkorn.
> *Best for:* Artisan boules for special occasions, bread that you want people to ask about, toast bread with honey and butter.
> *Handling notes:* Ancient grain gluten breaks down easily with overwork. Reduce mixing time by 30-40%. Use gentle stretch-and-fold rather than aggressive kneading. Shorten bulk fermentation by 20-25% compared to a pure bread flour dough — ancient grains ferment faster and the gluten degrades sooner. Shape with a light touch.

## Pro Tips

- **Read the protein label, not the brand.** Two bags labeled "bread flour" can differ by 2% in protein content, which is enough to change your dough's behavior completely. Train yourself to flip the bag and check.

- **Store whole grain flours in the freezer.** The oils in the bran and germ go rancid within a few weeks at room temperature. Freezing extends shelf life to several months. Let the flour come to room temperature before using it, or account for the temperature drop in your water.

- **Feed your starter what you bake with.** If your bread recipe uses 20% rye, feed your starter with the same blend for a few days before baking. This acclimates the microbial community to the flour it will encounter in the dough, producing a more vigorous and predictable fermentation.

- **Start small with new flours.** When experimenting, replace just 10% of your usual flour with the new one. Bake, evaluate, then increase in 10% increments. This way, you learn exactly what each flour contributes instead of changing everything at once and learning nothing.

- **Keep a flour journal.** Note the brand, protein content, and how your dough behaved. Over time, you will build a personal database that is more valuable than any book — because it reflects your water, your kitchen, and your hands.
