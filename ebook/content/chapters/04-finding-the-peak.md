# Finding the Peak

There is a moment in every feeding cycle when your starter is at its absolute best. It has consumed enough of the fresh flour to produce a surge of gas, its structure is taut with trapped carbon dioxide, and the wild yeast is at the height of its activity. Bakers call this the peak. It is the moment when your starter has the most leavening power, the most vitality, and the best balance of yeast and bacterial activity. Use your starter at peak, and your bread will rise with confidence. Miss the peak — use it too early or too late — and you are working with a weaker, less predictable culture.

The challenge is that peak is not a fixed point in time. It is a window, typically one to two hours wide, that shifts depending on temperature, flour type, feeding ratio, and the maturity of your culture. A starter fed 1:5:5 at 26 degrees Celsius might peak at hour eight. The same starter fed 1:2:2 at 22 degrees might peak at hour ten. There is no universal alarm clock. You have to learn to read the signs — and once you do, it becomes second nature. You will glance at your jar over morning coffee and know, without testing, whether it is ready.

This chapter teaches you to see what your starter is telling you. The visual cues, the tactile cues, the aromatic cues — they all converge at peak. Learn to recognize them, and you will never again wonder whether your starter is ready.

---

## The Five Signs of Peak

No single sign is definitive on its own. But when several of them align, you can be confident your starter is at or very near its peak.

**Volume.** A healthy starter at peak has doubled in volume from its post-feeding baseline — at minimum. Many vigorous starters triple or even quadruple. This is the most dramatic and easiest sign to read. Mark the level of your starter immediately after feeding (use a rubber band, a piece of tape, or a line drawn with a marker on the outside of the jar) and watch it climb over the following hours.

**The dome.** At peak, the surface of your starter will be gently domed — convex, rising slightly in the center. This dome tells you that gas production is still active and the structure is still holding. As your starter passes peak and begins to collapse, the dome flattens, then sinks. A flat or concave surface means you are past peak. Look at the dome as a real-time indicator: rising dome means still climbing, flat dome means peak, sinking dome means declining.

**Bubbles.** Before peak, you will see small bubbles forming throughout the starter, both on the surface and visible through the glass walls of the jar. At peak, these bubbles are at their most dense and varied — tiny pinprick bubbles alongside larger, irregular ones, creating an airy, sponge-like texture throughout the mass. The surface will look frothy and active. After peak, bubble activity decreases and the texture becomes more uniform and deflated.

**The jiggle.** Tap the side of your jar gently and watch how the starter responds. At peak, it jiggles like a soft, airy gelatin — bouncy, light, full of trapped gas. Before peak, it moves sluggishly, more like thick batter. After peak, it jiggles loosely, almost watery, as the structure collapses and gas escapes. This tactile test takes practice, but many experienced bakers rely on it more than any other sign.

**Aroma.** Smell your starter at different stages and you will learn to associate specific aromas with specific phases. Just after feeding, it smells floury and bland. As fermentation begins, a yeasty, bread-like sweetness develops. At peak, the aroma is pleasantly tangy — a balanced blend of yeast, mild sourness, and something almost fruity or wine-like. After peak, the aroma sharpens. The sweetness fades and is replaced by more aggressive acidity, sometimes with notes of nail polish remover (ethyl acetate) if the starter is very hungry. Trust your nose — it is more reliable than you think.

## The Float Test

The float test is the classic quick check. Scoop a small spoonful of your starter and drop it gently into a glass of room-temperature water. If it floats, the starter is full of gas and likely at or near peak. If it sinks, it has not yet generated enough gas — or it has already passed peak and lost its trapped air.

The float test is useful but not infallible. Very wet starters (100%+ hydration) sometimes fail the float test even at peak because their loose structure does not trap gas as effectively. Stiff starters almost always pass because their denser matrix holds gas well. Use the float test as one data point alongside the visual and aromatic signs, not as the sole arbiter.

> **A note on timing:** The float test tells you about the present moment. It does not tell you whether peak was ten minutes ago or an hour ago. A starter that barely floats may be on the way up (good) or on the way down (less good). Combine the float test with volume observation — if your starter has risen and is still domed, the float confirms you are in the peak window. If it has already begun to fall, the float test is less meaningful.

## What Happens After Peak

Understanding post-peak behavior helps you plan around it. After your starter crests and begins to decline, several things happen in sequence.

First, the dome collapses. Gas production slows as the yeast exhausts its food supply, and the structural network of gluten (in wheat-based starters) weakens under the acid load. The surface flattens, then sinks.

Next, the aroma sharpens. The mellow tang of peak gives way to a more pungent, boozy, or acetone-like smell. This is not dangerous — your starter is still alive and healthy — but it signals that the culture is hungry and the acid-to-yeast balance has shifted.

Finally, a layer of liquid may appear on the surface. This is **hooch** — a mix of alcohol and water produced by yeast fermentation. It is a harmless byproduct, and its appearance simply means your starter has been waiting too long for its next meal. Stir the hooch back in (or pour it off, if you prefer a milder flavor) and feed as usual.

Using a starter well past its peak is not catastrophic, but it will affect your bread. An over-ripe starter has less leavening power, higher acidity, and weaker gluten structure. Your dough may rise more slowly, taste more sour than intended, and produce a denser crumb. When possible, aim to use your starter within the peak window for the best results.

> **Deep Dive: The Peak Window**
>
> To truly understand peak, it helps to walk through a complete feeding cycle hour by hour. What follows is a representative timeline for a starter fed at 1:5:5 ratio with bread flour at 26 degrees Celsius. Your actual timing will vary, but the phases are universal.
>
> **Hour 0 — The Reset.** You have just fed your starter. The jar contains a smooth, homogeneous paste. There is no bubble activity yet. The aroma is plain flour and water. The starter sits at its baseline volume mark. Nothing appears to be happening, but beneath the surface, the small population of yeast and bacteria you carried over from the old starter is beginning to consume the fresh flour.
>
> **Hours 1-2 — The Lag Phase.** Still very little visible activity. You might see a few tiny bubbles forming at the edges of the jar, but volume has not changed. The microorganisms are adjusting to their new environment, beginning to produce enzymes that break starches into simple sugars. The aroma may develop a faintly sweet, floury quality. Patience is required here — many beginners worry that nothing is happening. Everything is happening; it is just invisible.
>
> **Hours 3-4 — Early Rise.** Now you can see it. Bubbles are forming throughout the starter, visible through the glass. Volume has increased by perhaps 25-50% from baseline. The surface is beginning to show a bubbly, uneven texture. The aroma has shifted from plain flour to something slightly yeasty and sweet, like raw bread dough. Fermentation has entered its exponential growth phase — the microbial population is doubling rapidly, and gas production is accelerating.
>
> **Hours 5-6 — The Climb.** Volume is now 75-100% above baseline. The starter is clearly rising. The dome is beginning to form — a gentle convexity on the surface. Bubbles are abundant, varied in size, and distributed throughout the mass. The aroma is yeasty, pleasantly tangy, with notes of ripe fruit. Tap the jar and the starter jiggles lightly, airy and full of gas. You are approaching the peak window, but you are not quite there yet. Using the starter now will work, but you would be leaving some leavening power on the table.
>
> **Hours 7-9 — The Peak Window.** This is it. Volume has doubled or more from baseline. The dome is full and rounded. The surface is a landscape of bubbles — frothy, active, alive. The aroma hits a perfect balance: tangy but sweet, yeasty but clean, complex but not sharp. The float test passes easily. The jiggle is soft and bouncy. The starter looks like it could rise forever, but in reality, it is approaching the limit of its food supply.
>
> This one-to-two-hour window is when your starter has the most leavening power and the best flavor balance for bread. If you are baking, this is when you mix your dough. If you are feeding again, this is when the culture is strongest to inoculate a new batch of flour.
>
> **Hours 10-11 — The Plateau and Early Decline.** The dome flattens. Volume may hold steady or begin to drop very slightly. Bubble activity on the surface slows — fewer new bubbles are forming, and existing ones are popping without being replaced. The aroma begins to sharpen, losing its sweet undertone. The starter is still usable at this stage, but its leavening power is diminishing. Bread made with a starter in early decline will still rise, but more slowly and with a tangier flavor.
>
> **Hour 12+ — Post-Peak Decline.** The surface has collapsed into a flat or concave shape. You may see a ring of dried starter on the jar walls above the current level — a high-water mark showing where the peak was. The aroma is pungent, sharp, possibly boozy. The jiggle is loose and watery. Hooch may appear. The starter is hungry and needs to be fed. If you use it now, expect weaker rise, more sour flavor, and a denser crumb.
>
> **What Shifts the Timeline**
>
> Everything described above assumes 26 degrees Celsius and a 1:5:5 ratio with bread flour. Change any variable and the timeline shifts. Higher temperature compresses the cycle — peak might arrive at hour 5 instead of hour 8. Lower temperature stretches it — peak at hour 12 or later. A higher feeding ratio (1:10:10) pushes peak even later. Whole grain flour accelerates the cycle because of its higher enzyme activity. Rye flour accelerates it dramatically. A more mature, well-established starter peaks faster than a young one because its microbial population is denser and more efficient.
>

## Pro Tips

- **Mark your jar at feeding time, every time.** A rubber band takes one second to place and gives you the single most reliable indicator of fermentation progress. Without a baseline mark, you are guessing at volume change.

- **Photograph your starter at peak.** Take a quick phone photo when you are confident your starter is at its best. Compare future feedings against that reference image. Your eye will calibrate faster with a visual benchmark.

- **Use the jiggle test when you are unsure.** The jiggle is harder to learn from descriptions than from practice, but once your hands know what a peak jiggle feels like — soft, bouncy, airy — you will reach for it instinctively. Tap the jar gently on the counter and watch the response.

- **Plan backwards from your bake.** If you want to mix your dough at 8 AM and your starter typically peaks 8 hours after feeding, feed it at midnight. If that is inconvenient, adjust the ratio or temperature to shift the peak window to a time that works for you. The starter serves your schedule, not the other way around.

- **Do not chase perfection.** Using your starter 30 minutes before or after the exact peak will not ruin your bread. The peak window is forgiving. What matters is avoiding the extremes — do not use a starter that has barely begun to rise, and do not use one that collapsed hours ago. Anywhere within the window produces excellent bread.
