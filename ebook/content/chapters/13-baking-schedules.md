# Baking Schedules

The question that stops most people from baking sourdough is not about technique — it is about time. How can something that takes eighteen hours fit into a life already full of work, family, and everything else? The answer is simpler than you think: sourdough does not need eighteen hours of your attention. It needs eighteen hours of existence, during which you spend roughly thirty to forty-five minutes doing actual work. The rest is waiting — and most of that waiting can happen while you sleep, while you commute, while you live your life.

The secret weapon in every sourdough baker's arsenal is the refrigerator. Cold slows fermentation to a crawl without stopping it. This means you can press pause on your bread at almost any stage, walk away for hours or even days, and pick up exactly where you left off. A dough that would overproof in four hours at room temperature will hold beautifully in the fridge for twelve, twenty-four, or even thirty-six hours. This transforms sourdough from a demanding taskmaster into something remarkably flexible.

What you need is not more free time — you need the right schedule. One that fits around your commitments, not the other way around. In this chapter, you will find timelines designed for real lives, along with the knowledge to adapt them when plans inevitably change.

---

## The Weekend Baker

This is the most popular schedule for people who work weekdays and want fresh bread on Saturday. The work is spread across three days, but the actual hands-on time is minimal.

**Thursday evening:** Feed your starter. If it has been in the fridge, take it out and give it a 1:2:2 feeding with room-temperature water. Leave it on the counter overnight.

**Friday morning or afternoon:** Your starter should be active and bubbly, near its peak or recently peaked. Mix your dough — combine flour, water, starter, and salt. Over the next two hours, perform three to four sets of stretch and folds, spaced thirty minutes apart. This is your most active window, but each set of folds takes less than a minute.

**Friday evening:** Once the bulk fermentation looks right (the dough has grown by roughly fifty percent and feels airy), pre-shape, bench rest, and do your final shaping. Place the shaped loaf in a floured banneton, cover it, and slide it into the refrigerator. Go to bed.

**Saturday morning:** Preheat your oven with your Dutch oven inside — this takes forty-five minutes to an hour at maximum heat. Take the cold dough straight from the fridge, score it, and bake. The cold proof firms the dough beautifully, making it easy to score with precision. Fresh bread by mid-morning.

## The Overnight Bake

This schedule compresses everything into roughly eighteen hours, starting one morning and finishing the next. It works well for people who have a free morning to mix dough and want bread the following day.

**Day one, morning (8:00-9:00):** Feed your starter first thing. By early afternoon, it will be at peak activity.

**Day one, afternoon (14:00-15:00):** Mix your dough. Over the next two to three hours, perform your stretch and folds while going about your afternoon.

**Day one, evening (19:00-20:00):** When bulk fermentation is complete, shape the dough and place it in the fridge for an overnight cold proof.

**Day two, morning (7:00-8:00):** Preheat oven and Dutch oven. Score and bake the cold loaf. Fresh bread before you leave for the day.

The beauty of this schedule is that the cold proof is doing the work while you sleep. The slow, cold fermentation also develops more complex flavors — many bakers find that an overnight proof produces bread with more depth and a more open crumb than a same-day bake.

## Tips for Busy Schedules

**The fridge is your pause button.** This cannot be overstated. If life interrupts — the phone rings, a child needs you, something comes up — slide your dough into the refrigerator. Bulk fermentation slows to nearly nothing. Shaped dough holds for twelve to thirty-six hours cold. You can resume when you are ready.

**Starter flexibility is real.** Your starter does not need to be fed at the exact same time every day. A mature, healthy starter can go twenty-four hours between feedings without any harm. If you keep it in the fridge, it can go a week or more between feedings. Do not let starter maintenance become a source of stress — it is far more resilient than you think.

**Cold proofing improves results.** What starts as a scheduling convenience becomes a technique you choose deliberately. Cold-proofed loaves hold their shape better, score more cleanly, and develop a more complex flavor profile. Many professional bakers cold-proof every single loaf regardless of schedule.

> **If you only remember one thing:** Your total active time — hands actually touching dough — is thirty to forty-five minutes per loaf. Everything else is passive. Sourdough fits into busy lives not by demanding less time, but by demanding less of your attention.

> **Deep Dive: 5 Complete Baking Timelines**
>
> These five schedules cover every lifestyle. Each one is a complete, hour-by-hour plan from feeding your starter to pulling bread from the oven. Pay attention to the total active time — you will notice it is remarkably consistent across all five schedules, usually thirty to forty-five minutes. The difference is when that time falls in your day.
>
> **1. The Early Bird**
> *Fresh bread by noon. For early risers and morning people.*
>
> **The day before, 20:00** — Feed your starter (1:2:2 ratio). Leave on counter overnight.
> **05:00** — Check starter. It should be peaked or just past peak. Mix dough: combine flour, water, starter, salt. Autolyse for 30 minutes.
> **05:30** — Incorporate salt. Begin stretch and fold sets.
> **05:30-07:30** — Perform 4 sets of stretch and folds, 30 minutes apart (4 minutes total active time).
> **07:30-09:30** — Bulk fermentation continues. Monitor the dough — you are looking for a 50% volume increase, a domed surface, and visible bubbles at the edges.
> **09:30** — Pre-shape. Bench rest 20 minutes.
> **09:50** — Final shape. Place in banneton.
> **09:55** — Preheat oven with Dutch oven to maximum (250 degrees Celsius).
> **10:00-11:00** — Final proof at room temperature while oven heats (this short warm proof is sufficient because the dough is already well-fermented).
> **11:00** — Score and bake. 20 minutes lidded, 20-25 minutes open.
> **11:45** — Bread is out. Cool on wire rack.
>
> Total active time: approximately 35 minutes. Bread ready by noon.
> **Rescue plan:** If the bulk fermentation is not ready by 09:30, the dough is moving slowly — your kitchen may be cold. Give it more time. If you need to leave, refrigerate the dough and shape it when you return. You can bake in the afternoon instead.
>
> **2. The 9-to-5er**
> *Mix after work, bake the next morning. For people with conventional work schedules.*
>
> **Day 1, 07:00** — Feed your starter before leaving for work (1:2:2 ratio). Leave on counter.
> **Day 1, 17:30** — Arrive home. Starter should be at or near peak. Mix dough immediately.
> **Day 1, 18:00** — First stretch and fold.
> **Day 1, 18:00-20:00** — Perform 3-4 sets of stretch and folds during dinner and evening routine. Each set takes under a minute.
> **Day 1, 21:00-22:00** — Bulk fermentation should be complete (check for 50% rise). Pre-shape, bench rest, final shape.
> **Day 1, 22:00** — Place shaped loaf in banneton in the refrigerator. Go to bed.
> **Day 2, 06:00** — Preheat oven with Dutch oven to maximum.
> **Day 2, 06:45** — Score cold loaf straight from fridge and bake. 20 minutes lidded, 20-25 minutes open.
> **Day 2, 07:30** — Bread is out. Cool while you get ready for work.
>
> Total active time: approximately 40 minutes. Fresh bread before work.
> **Rescue plan:** If the dough is not ready by bedtime (kitchen was cold, starter was sluggish), refrigerate it wherever it is in bulk fermentation. The next evening, take it out, let it finish bulking at room temperature for an hour or two, then shape and refrigerate again. Bake on Day 3 morning. The fridge bought you a full extra day.
>
> **3. The Weekend Warrior**
> *Prep Friday night, bake Saturday morning. The most relaxed schedule.*
>
> **Thursday evening, 20:00** — Take starter from fridge (if stored cold). Feed 1:2:2. Leave on counter.
> **Friday, 08:00** — Feed starter again (1:2:2) if it peaked overnight and has started to fall. This second feed ensures maximum vigor.
> **Friday, 17:00-18:00** — Starter at peak. Mix dough. Autolyse 30 minutes. Add salt.
> **Friday, 18:00-20:00** — Stretch and folds during Friday evening, 30 minutes apart. Four sets.
> **Friday, 22:00-23:00** — Bulk fermentation complete. Pre-shape, bench rest, final shape.
> **Friday, 23:00** — Banneton into the fridge. Go to bed.
> **Saturday, 08:00** — Preheat oven with Dutch oven.
> **Saturday, 08:45** — Score and bake. 20 minutes lidded, 20-25 minutes open.
> **Saturday, 09:30** — Bread out. Cool on wire rack. Fresh bread for a Saturday morning.
>
> Total active time: approximately 40 minutes spread over two days. No rushing.
> **Rescue plan:** If Saturday morning does not work, leave the dough in the fridge. It will hold until Sunday morning without any problem — the extra cold time deepens flavor. If you cannot bake Sunday either, the dough can hold up to 36 hours cold before overproofing becomes a concern.
>
> **4. The Night Owl**
> *Mix late at night, bake the next afternoon. For people who come alive after dark.*
>
> **Evening, 18:00** — Feed your starter (1:2:2). Let it rise.
> **22:00-23:00** — Starter at peak. Mix dough. Autolyse 30 minutes. Add salt.
> **23:00-01:00** — Stretch and folds during your late-night hours. Three to four sets.
> **01:00** — Slide the bowl of dough into the refrigerator for a cold bulk fermentation. Go to sleep.
> **Next day, 11:00-12:00** — Take dough from fridge. It will have fermented slowly overnight in the cold. Let it warm at room temperature for 30-60 minutes.
> **12:00-12:30** — Pre-shape, bench rest, final shape.
> **12:30** — Place in banneton. Final proof at room temperature for 1.5-2 hours. Preheat oven during this time.
> **14:30** — Score and bake. 20 minutes lidded, 20-25 minutes open.
> **15:15** — Bread out. Fresh loaf for an afternoon meal.
>
> Total active time: approximately 45 minutes. Baking fits into a late riser's day.
> **Rescue plan:** If the dough looks under-fermented when you take it out (hasn't grown much in the fridge), leave it at room temperature longer before shaping — it just needs more time. If it looks over-fermented (very bubbly, loose, spreading), shape immediately and bake quickly. A slightly over-proofed loaf still tastes excellent even if the oven spring is modest.
>
> **5. The Busy Parent**
> *Maximum fridge time, minimum active time. For people who can only spare moments.*
>
> **Day 1, any time** — Feed starter. Ignore it until it peaks (this is fine — a fed starter sitting on the counter is low-maintenance).
> **Day 1, whenever starter peaks** — Mix dough in under five minutes. Flour, water, starter, salt. Stir until combined. Do not even autolyse if time is short.
> **Day 1, next 1-2 hours** — Perform two sets of stretch and folds when you walk through the kitchen. That is it. Two sets.
> **Day 1, whenever convenient** — Put the bowl in the fridge. The dough will bulk ferment in the cold over the next 12-24 hours. You do not need to monitor it.
> **Day 2, whenever convenient** — Take the dough out. It will be cold, slightly puffed, and easy to handle. Pre-shape on the counter, bench rest 15-20 minutes, final shape, place in banneton.
> **Day 2, option A** — Put the banneton back in the fridge. Bake tomorrow. Yes, this is three days total, and the bread will be fantastic.
> **Day 2, option B** — Proof at room temperature for 1.5-2 hours, then bake.
> **Bake day** — Preheat oven 45 minutes. Score cold dough. Bake 20 minutes lidded, 20-25 minutes open.
>
> Total active time: approximately 30 minutes spread over two to three days. The fridge does all the heavy lifting.
> **Rescue plan:** Forgot about the dough in the fridge? If it has been 48 hours, check it. If it still has some structure and spring when poked, bake it — it may be slightly over-proofed but will still taste wonderful (and more sour, which some people prefer). If it has completely collapsed and smells strongly of alcohol, it has gone too far. Use it as a high-hydration discard for pizza dough, focaccia, or pancakes, where overproofing is less critical.

## Pro Tips

- **Write your schedule down the first few times.** Pick one of the timelines above, adjust the clock times to your life, and write it on a sticky note. After three or four bakes on the same schedule, it becomes second nature and you will not need the note.

- **Track your kitchen temperature.** A kitchen at 22 degrees Celsius behaves very differently from one at 27 degrees. If your timelines are consistently off — dough ready too early or too late — your ambient temperature is the reason. Adjust by shortening or lengthening bulk fermentation, or use warmer or cooler water in your mix.

- **Double down on cold proofing.** If there is one technique that makes scheduling easier, it is the overnight cold proof after shaping. It decouples shaping from baking entirely. Shape when convenient, bake when convenient, and the bread is better for it.

- **Batch your bakes.** Once you are comfortable with a schedule, make two loaves instead of one. The active time barely increases — you are already measuring, mixing, and heating the oven. Bake them back to back or freeze the second shaped dough for up to two weeks.

- **Let go of perfection on timing.** An extra hour of bulk fermentation or an extra six hours of cold proofing rarely ruins bread. Sourdough has wide margins. The timelines above are guides, not rigid prescriptions. Trust the dough more than the clock.
