# Shaping & Scoring

There is a moment in every bake when the dough stops being an amorphous mass and becomes a loaf. That moment is shaping. Everything you have done up to this point — the careful feeding of your starter, the patient bulk fermentation, the stretch and folds — has been preparation. Now you give the dough its identity. You decide whether it will be a taut, round boule or an elegant oval batard. You build the surface tension that will hold the loaf together as it rises one final time, and then you score it — a deliberate act of controlled violence that determines how your bread opens in the oven.

Shaping and scoring are where artistry meets engineering. A well-shaped loaf with confident scoring will spring upward in the oven, splitting along your blade marks with dramatic, caramelized ridges. A poorly shaped loaf, no matter how perfect the fermentation, will spread sideways, tear at random, and bake flat. These are physical skills, which means they improve with practice. Your first few loaves will look rough. Your tenth will be noticeably better. Your fiftieth will make you proud.

The good news is that the techniques themselves are straightforward. You do not need expensive equipment — a bench scraper, a clean surface, and a sharp blade are all that stands between you and a beautifully formed loaf.

---

## Pre-Shaping

Pre-shaping is the often-overlooked step that makes final shaping dramatically easier. Think of it as a rough draft. You are not trying to create the finished form — you are organizing the dough, building initial tension, and giving the gluten a chance to relax before the final shape.

Turn your dough out onto a lightly floured surface. Using a bench scraper, slide the blade under one edge of the dough and fold it toward the center. Rotate the mass ninety degrees and repeat. Do this four or five times until you have a rough ball. The dough should not be perfectly smooth — just gathered and slightly taut on top.

Now leave it alone. Cover it loosely with a clean towel or an inverted bowl and let it rest for fifteen to twenty-five minutes. This is the bench rest, and it is not optional. During this time, the gluten relaxes. If you skip straight to final shaping, the dough will fight you — it will spring back, resist stretching, and tear rather than cooperate. After a proper bench rest, the dough becomes supple and willing. You will feel the difference in your hands.

> **A note on flour:** Use just enough flour to prevent sticking during pre-shaping, but not so much that you lose friction. The dough needs to grip the work surface slightly — that gentle drag is what builds surface tension. If your dough slides around like it is on ice, you have used too much flour.

## Final Shaping

For a round loaf (boule), the most forgiving shape for beginners, begin with your pre-shaped round sitting seam-side up on a lightly floured surface. Mentally divide the dough into compass points. Take the north edge and fold it to the center. Take the south edge and fold it over. Repeat with east and west. Now flip the dough over so the seam is on the bottom and the smooth side faces up.

Here is where surface tension is built. Place both hands around the back of the dough, pinkies touching the work surface. Pull the dough gently toward you. The bottom of the dough should grip the unfloured surface while the top stretches taut. Rotate the dough a quarter turn and pull again. Repeat three or four times, rotating each time, until the surface is visibly tight — smooth, drum-like, with no slack or wrinkles. You should see the dough holding its shape confidently, not slumping.

Transfer the shaped loaf, seam-side up, into a well-floured proofing basket (banneton). The seam will become the top of your loaf after you turn it out for baking. Dust the top lightly with rice flour, cover, and move to the refrigerator for your cold proof or let it proof at room temperature, depending on your schedule.

**Surface tension is everything.** Without it, your loaf will spread flat in the oven. Too much tension and the surface will tear during proofing. You are looking for the sweet spot — a taut skin that stretches smoothly without ripping. If the dough tears, you have overworked it. Let it rest five minutes and try one gentle tightening pass.

## Scoring

Scoring is the final act before the oven. When bread bakes, the rapid expansion of steam and gas inside the dough needs somewhere to go. If you do not score, the loaf will burst at its weakest point — an unpredictable, often ugly blowout. Scoring gives you control. You decide where the bread opens, how it opens, and what pattern the crust reveals.

A razor blade held in a lame (a curved scoring tool) is the preferred instrument. A sharp serrated knife will work in a pinch, but nothing matches the precision of a fresh razor blade. Blades dull surprisingly fast — change yours every three to five bakes.

**Depth matters.** For most patterns, you want cuts approximately half a centimeter to one centimeter deep. Shallow cuts seal shut during baking and accomplish nothing. Cuts that are too deep can cause the loaf to collapse or open so wide that the crumb dries out.

**Angle matters.** For the classic ear — that dramatic ridge of crust that curls back from the slash — hold your blade at roughly a thirty-degree angle to the surface of the dough, almost parallel. A vertical cut produces a symmetrical opening, which is fine for cross patterns but will not create an ear.

**Speed matters.** Score with a single, confident, swift motion. Hesitation causes the blade to drag and catch on the wet dough, producing a ragged line. Think of it as a brushstroke — decisive and fluid.

> **Common Mistakes**
>
> **Too much flour on top.** A heavy coat of flour before scoring makes the blade skip and slide. Dust lightly, or brush excess flour away from where you intend to cut.
>
> **Over-handling after shaping.** If you reshape after building tension, you lose everything you built. Shape once, score once, bake.
>
> **Shallow scoring.** The single most common beginner error. If your cuts disappear during baking, you did not go deep enough. Commit to the cut.
>
> **Too-deep scoring.** Less common but equally problematic. Cuts deeper than one centimeter can destabilize the loaf's structure and cause it to flatten as gas escapes uncontrollably.

---

> **Deep Dive: Scoring Pattern Guide**
>
> Scoring is where your bread becomes yours. Beyond the functional purpose of controlling oven spring, scoring is a canvas for expression. These eight classic patterns range from the simplest single slash to freeform artistry. Each one changes how the loaf opens, how the crust forms, and how the finished bread looks on your table.
>
> **1. Single Slash (The Classic Ear)**
> Difficulty: Beginner. Best for: round boules and oval batards.
> This is the most iconic sourdough score and the one you should master first. Hold your lame at a thirty-degree angle to the dough surface, blade nearly parallel. Starting about two centimeters from the top of the loaf, draw a single swift line down the length, extending to about two centimeters from the bottom. The cut should be roughly half a centimeter deep and slightly off-center — not directly across the middle but offset to one side. This asymmetry is what creates the ear. During baking, the scored side lifts and peels back while the uncut side pushes outward, forming that coveted ridge of blistered, caramelized crust. Use a razor blade on a curved lame for the best control. Speed and confidence are everything here — one clean stroke.
>
> **2. Cross Score**
> Difficulty: Beginner. Best for: round boules.
> Two perpendicular cuts across the top of a round loaf, forming a plus sign. Hold the blade vertically (ninety degrees to the surface) rather than at an angle. Each cut should span about two-thirds of the loaf's diameter and go roughly half a centimeter deep. The cross score produces a symmetrical, even opening in four directions. It is forgiving because there is no precise angle to worry about — vertical cuts are simpler to execute. The resulting loaf has a rustic, farmhouse appearance with four petals of crust pulling apart evenly. This pattern works beautifully with a heavy dusting of flour on top for contrast.
>
> **3. Square / Box Pattern**
> Difficulty: Beginner to Intermediate. Best for: round boules.
> Score a square on the top of the loaf — four straight cuts forming a box, each side roughly eight to ten centimeters long. Hold the blade vertically and cut about half a centimeter deep. The center section inside the square lifts during baking, creating a raised plateau surrounded by a crusty frame. For a more dramatic effect, slightly deepen the corners where the cuts meet. Some bakers offset the square so it sits like a diamond (rotated forty-five degrees), which changes the visual dynamic entirely. A serrated knife or straight razor works better than a curved lame for the clean straight lines this pattern demands.
>
> **4. Wheat Stalk / Leaf**
> Difficulty: Intermediate. Best for: oval batards.
> This decorative pattern mimics a wheat stalk or leaf running down the length of the loaf. First, score a single straight line down the center of the loaf as your stem — a shallow guide cut, just a few millimeters deep. Then, working from the top down, make a series of short diagonal cuts branching off each side of the stem, alternating left and right. Each branch cut should be two to three centimeters long, angled at roughly forty-five degrees from the stem, and half a centimeter deep. Space them about one and a half centimeters apart. The key is consistency in angle and spacing. Use a razor blade on a straight lame or held directly between your fingers for maximum precision. The leaf pattern is visually stunning but purely decorative — the shallow cuts do not significantly affect oven spring, so consider adding one deeper functional cut along the side or bottom of the loaf to direct expansion.
>
> **5. Spiral**
> Difficulty: Intermediate to Advanced. Best for: round boules.
> Beginning at the center of the loaf, score a continuous curved line that spirals outward toward the edge, making two to three full rotations. Hold the blade at a slight angle (about forty-five degrees) and cut approximately half a centimeter deep. The trick is maintaining consistent depth and spacing as you rotate — the gap between each ring of the spiral should be roughly two centimeters. Move your hand in a steady, fluid arc. The spiral produces an extraordinary visual when the loaf opens: the entire surface unfurls in a pinwheel of crust. Rotate the dough on a turntable or lazy Susan if you have one — it makes the continuous curve far easier to execute. A curved lame follows the arc naturally and is the ideal tool here.
>
> **6. Crescent Moon**
> Difficulty: Intermediate. Best for: round boules and oval batards.
> A single curved cut that arcs across one side of the loaf, creating an asymmetric crescent opening. The technique is similar to the single slash but follows a curve. Hold your lame at a thirty-degree angle, start at one side of the loaf, and sweep in a smooth arc to the other side, creating a crescent shape. Cut approximately half a centimeter deep. The curved line creates an elegant, sweeping ear that opens wider at the center of the arc and tapers at the ends. The crescent works particularly well on loaves with a heavy flour dusting — the contrast between the dark, blistered interior of the cut and the white flour is striking. Use a curved lame and let the natural arc of the blade guide your hand.
>
> **7. Hashtag / Grid**
> Difficulty: Intermediate. Best for: round boules and large miche loaves.
> Score two parallel vertical lines and two parallel horizontal lines across the top, creating a grid of nine sections. Hold the blade vertically and cut each line about half a centimeter deep. Space the parallel lines roughly three to four centimeters apart. The grid pattern opens evenly in multiple directions, producing a loaf with a deeply textured, almost cobblestone-like crust. Each square section puffs up individually during baking. For a refined look, make the cuts precise and evenly spaced. For a more rustic appearance, allow slight irregularity. A straight razor or sharp knife gives cleaner lines than a curved lame. This pattern is excellent for large loaves where a single slash would not provide enough expansion relief.
>
> **8. Free-Form Artistic**
> Difficulty: Advanced. Best for: any shape, once you are confident with basics.
> This is not a specific pattern but a philosophy: use the blade as a drawing tool. Floral designs, geometric tessellations, portraits, landscapes — the dough is your canvas. The technical principles remain the same: consistent depth (three to five millimeters for decorative lines, deeper for functional expansion lines), swift strokes, and a fresh blade. The key insight for artistic scoring is to separate your decorative cuts from your functional cuts. Make one or two deep functional scores on the side or bottom of the loaf where they will not be visible, allowing the bread to expand there. Then use shallow decorative cuts on the top surface, knowing they will open just enough to create contrast without distorting your design. Bread scissors (lames ciseaux) are invaluable for snipping small pointed cuts that a flat blade cannot achieve — think of the peaked cuts on an epi baguette or small triangular lifts in a decorative pattern.

## Pro Tips

- **Wet your bench scraper, not the dough.** During pre-shaping, a damp bench scraper slides under the dough without dragging. This gives you clean folds without adding flour, preserving the surface tack you need for building tension.

- **Shape on an unfloured surface for the final round.** It sounds counterintuitive, but the gentle friction between the dough and a clean, unfloured countertop is exactly what creates surface tension. The dough grips slightly, and when you drag it toward you, the top stretches taut. Flour eliminates that friction.

- **Chill before scoring.** If your dough is warm and sticky after shaping, refrigerate it for twenty to thirty minutes before scoring. Cold dough holds its shape under the blade and produces cleaner, more defined cuts. Many bakers score straight from an overnight cold proof for precisely this reason.

- **One blade, one bake.** Razor blades are cheap. A dull blade drags, tears, and sticks. If you bake once a week, a single blade might last three or four sessions. If you notice the blade catching rather than gliding, replace it immediately. The difference is dramatic.

- **Score with your whole arm, not your fingers.** Small, tentative wrist movements produce shaky, uneven lines. Anchor your hand, take a breath, and draw the blade in one smooth motion using your entire forearm. Think calligraphy, not surgery.
