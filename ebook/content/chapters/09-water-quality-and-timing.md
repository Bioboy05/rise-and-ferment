# Water Quality & Timing

There is a quiet irony in sourdough baking: you obsess over flour brands, starter ratios, and oven temperatures, yet the single ingredient you use most — water — rarely gets a second thought. You turn on the tap, fill a cup, and pour it in. But water is not just a vehicle for hydration. It is an active participant in fermentation, carrying minerals that feed your culture, chemicals that can suppress it, and a temperature that sets the tempo of every biological process in your jar.

The truth is that many struggling bakers could transform their starter overnight simply by changing what comes out of their faucet. Not with expensive equipment or exotic spring water shipped from the Alps, but with a few minutes of understanding and a simple adjustment. Water quality and water temperature are two of the most underestimated levers you have — and once you learn to use them, you gain a level of control over your fermentation that feels almost unfair.

This chapter will change the way you think about every drop that enters your starter jar.

---

## The Chlorine Problem

Municipal water systems add disinfectants to keep drinking water safe, and those same disinfectants are remarkably effective at killing microorganisms — including the wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria you are trying to cultivate. The two most common disinfectants are chlorine and chloramine, and they behave very differently.

**Chlorine** is volatile. It wants to escape into the air, which means you can remove it simply by leaving a jug of water on the counter uncovered for twelve to twenty-four hours. You can speed this up by boiling the water for five minutes and letting it cool. If your tap water smells faintly of a swimming pool, you almost certainly have chlorine, and it is the easier of the two to deal with.

**Chloramine**, on the other hand, is chlorine bonded to ammonia. This bond makes it far more stable — it does not evaporate, and boiling alone will not remove it. Chloramine has become increasingly common in water treatment because of that stability. If your water has chloramine, your best options are a carbon-block or catalytic carbon filter, or using bottled spring water. A standard Brita-style pitcher filter can reduce chloramine, but a dedicated carbon-block filter under your sink is more reliable.

> **How do you know which one you have?** Call your water utility or check their annual water quality report, which is usually available online. Search for your city name plus "water quality report" and look for the disinfectant section. This one phone call or search can save you months of frustration.

## Temperature: The Tempo Knob

If water quality determines whether your microorganisms survive, water temperature determines how fast they work. Think of temperature as the tempo knob on a metronome — turn it up and everything accelerates, turn it down and everything slows to a crawl.

**The standard range (25-30°C / 77-86°F)** is your everyday feeding temperature. Water in this range produces a balanced fermentation where lactic acid bacteria and wild yeast work in harmony. Your starter will peak predictably, the flavor will be moderately tangy with pleasant complexity, and you will develop an intuitive sense of its rhythm within a few days.

**The boost range (30-35°C / 86-95°F)** accelerates fermentation noticeably. Use warmer water when your starter seems sluggish, when your kitchen is cold, or when you need it to peak faster for a baking schedule. Be aware that faster fermentation tends to favor lactic acid bacteria over yeast, which can produce a milder, creamier flavor profile. Above 35°C, you risk stressing or killing your yeast — so treat this as an upper boundary, not a target.

**The slow lane (below 20°C / 68°F)** puts the brakes on everything. Cool water and cool environments slow fermentation dramatically, which can be useful when you want to delay a feeding or develop more acetic acid (the sharp, vinegary tang). But if your kitchen is consistently below 20°C and you are using cold tap water on top of that, your starter may appear nearly dead. In winter, simply using slightly warm water — around 28°C — can be the difference between a thriving culture and a sluggish one.

> **A quick rule of thumb:** if your kitchen is cold, use warmer water. If your kitchen is warm, room-temperature water is fine. You are aiming for a dough temperature after mixing of roughly 25-28°C. The water is your easiest tool for hitting that target.

## Feeding Schedule and the Art of Observation

New bakers often ask, "How often should I feed my starter?" and expect a precise answer — every twelve hours, every twenty-four hours, twice a day. But the honest answer is more nuanced: **feed your starter when it needs feeding, not when the clock says so.**

A healthy starter tells you when it is hungry. It rises, peaks, and then begins to fall. The surface, once domed and buoyant, flattens and may develop a thin layer of liquid called hooch. The aroma shifts from pleasantly yeasty and tangy to something sharper, almost alcoholic. These are the signs. Learn to read them, and you will never need a timer.

That said, consistency matters. Your microbial community thrives on routine. If you feed at roughly the same time each day, using the same ratio and the same water temperature, you create a stable environment where the strongest, most reliable strains of yeast and bacteria dominate. Erratic feeding — skipping a day here, doubling the flour there — creates chaos in the jar, and chaos produces inconsistent results.

The phrase bakers love to repeat is **"watch your starter, not the clock."** It sounds like a platitude, but it encodes a deep truth: every starter is unique, every kitchen is different, and every season shifts the timeline. A starter in a 30°C kitchen in July will peak in four hours. The same starter in a 16°C kitchen in January might take fourteen. The clock cannot account for this. Your eyes and nose can.

> **Deep Dive**
>
> ## The Water Testing Protocol
>
> If you suspect your water might be holding your starter back, this protocol will give you a definitive answer. Set aside one weekend, gather a few inexpensive supplies, and run the experiment. The results will either confirm that your water is fine or reveal the exact adjustment you need to make.
>
> **Step 1: Identify Your Disinfectant**
>
> Before you test anything at home, find out what your water utility uses. Visit their website and download the most recent Consumer Confidence Report (also called an Annual Water Quality Report). Look for the "Disinfectant" or "Disinfection" section. It will list either chlorine (free chlorine) or chloramine (combined chlorine, or monochloramine). Write down the concentration — it is usually reported in milligrams per liter (mg/L) or parts per million (ppm). Levels below 0.5 mg/L are generally low enough that most starters tolerate them. Levels above 2.0 mg/L warrant action.
>
> **Step 2: The Smell and Sit Test**
>
> Fill a glass with cold tap water, smell it, and note any pool-like odor. Then fill an open jar with tap water and leave it on the counter, uncovered, for twenty-four hours. Smell it again. If the chlorine smell is gone, your water has chlorine (not chloramine), and simply letting water sit before using it is sufficient. If it still smells the same, you likely have chloramine and will need filtration.
>
> **Step 3: The pH Check**
>
> Pick up a pack of pH test strips from a pharmacy or aquarium store. Test your tap water — most municipal water falls between 6.5 and 8.5. Water on the alkaline side (above 7.5) can slightly buffer the acids your starter produces, potentially slowing its development. This is rarely a dealbreaker, but it is worth knowing. Ideal water for sourdough sits between 6.5 and 7.5.
>
> **Step 4: The Three-Jar Experiment**
>
> This is the heart of the protocol. You will maintain three identical mini-starters side by side for five days, changing only the water source.
>
> Label three small jars: **Tap**, **Filtered**, and **Bottled**. Use the same flour for all three (a simple bread flour works best). On day one, add 20 grams of flour and 20 grams of the designated water to each jar. Stir, cover loosely, and place them side by side in the same location so temperature is identical.
>
> Each morning, discard all but 10 grams from each jar and feed with 20 grams flour and 20 grams of the appropriate water. At peak activity each day (you will learn to spot it), note three things: how high it rose (mark the jar with a rubber band at the starting level), how it smells, and how many hours it took to peak.
>
> By day three or four, differences will emerge if your tap water is problematic. The tap jar may lag behind in rise height or take significantly longer to peak. If all three jars perform similarly, your tap water is perfectly fine — save your money on bottled water and use it for something else.
>
> **Step 5: Mineral Content — Hard vs. Soft Water**
>
> Your water quality report also lists hardness, usually in mg/L of calcium carbonate. Moderately hard water (60-120 mg/L) is generally ideal for sourdough. The minerals provide nutrients for fermentation and help strengthen gluten structure. Very soft water (below 30 mg/L) can produce a slacker, stickier dough, while very hard water (above 180 mg/L) can tighten gluten excessively and slow fermentation. If your water is very soft, a tiny pinch of food-grade mineral supplement or a splash of mineral-rich bottled water can help. If very hard, a simple carbon filter will not change hardness — you would need a specific mineral-adjusting filter, though this is rarely necessary for starters.
>
> **Water Quality Checklist**
>
> - Disinfectant type identified (chlorine or chloramine)
> - Concentration noted (below 0.5 mg/L is low concern)
> - Smell test performed (before and after 24-hour sit)
> - pH tested (target: 6.5-7.5)
> - Hardness checked (sweet spot: 60-120 mg/L)
> - Three-jar experiment completed over 5 days
> - Decision made: tap is fine, or switch to filtered/bottled

## Pro Tips

- **Winter rescue:** When your kitchen drops below 18°C, use 30°C water for feedings and place your starter on top of the refrigerator or near (not on) a radiator. The combination of warm water and a slightly warmer microclimate can cut peak time in half.

- **The thermos trick:** If you do not have a thermometer, run your tap until the water feels comfortably warm on the inside of your wrist — like testing a baby's bottle. That sensation corresponds roughly to 28-32°C, which is a perfectly serviceable range for feeding.

- **Dechlorinate in bulk:** If you have chlorine (not chloramine), fill a large pitcher each evening and leave it uncovered on the counter. By morning, the chlorine has off-gassed and you have ready-to-use water for your feeding without any delay.

- **Consistency beats perfection:** Using the same water source at roughly the same temperature every day matters more than finding the theoretically "perfect" water. Your microbial community adapts to its environment — give it a stable one.

- **Travel-proof your routine:** When visiting family or traveling, bring a small bag of your usual flour and buy a bottle of spring water at your destination. Unfamiliar tap water is the number-one reason starters act strangely on the road.
