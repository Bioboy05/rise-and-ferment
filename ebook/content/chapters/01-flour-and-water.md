# Flour & Water: The Basics

Every sourdough starter begins with an act of faith. You take two of the simplest ingredients on Earth — flour and water — and you mix them together, trusting that invisible organisms will transform this paste into something alive. It sounds almost too simple, and yet the quality of those two ingredients shapes everything that follows. The flour you choose determines how fast your starter develops, how it tastes, and how it performs in bread. The water you use can either nourish your culture or quietly sabotage it.

Experienced bakers will tell you that sourdough is forgiving. And it is — eventually. But in those first critical days, when you are coaxing wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria out of dormancy, the details matter more than you might expect. A flour with the wrong protein content can leave you wondering why nothing is happening. Tap water heavy with chlorine can stall fermentation before it begins. Understanding these fundamentals does not make you fussy — it makes you effective.

This chapter is about giving your starter the best possible foundation. Once you understand why certain flours behave the way they do and what your water actually contains, you will make better decisions instinctively. You will stop guessing and start observing with purpose.

---

## Why Flour Type Matters

Flour is not just food for your starter — it is the ecosystem itself. The wild yeast and bacteria you are cultivating already live on the grain, nestled into the bran and the outer layers of the wheat berry. When you choose a flour, you are choosing which microbial populations get a seat at the table.

**Whole grain flours** — whole wheat, dark rye, spelt — carry far more wild organisms than refined white flours. They also contain more minerals and enzymes that accelerate fermentation. This is why most guides recommend starting with rye or whole wheat: you are stacking the deck in your favor. Rye flour in particular is extraordinarily enzyme-active. Even a small proportion of rye (10-20% of your total flour) can jumpstart a sluggish culture.

**White bread flour**, with its higher protein content (11.5-13%), gives your starter excellent structure once it matures. The gluten network traps gas efficiently, producing the dramatic rise that tells you fermentation is healthy. Many bakers settle on bread flour for daily maintenance feedings after the initial creation phase.

**All-purpose flour** works, but its lower protein content (9.5-11.5%) means less gluten development and a slightly thinner consistency. If all-purpose is what you have, use it without hesitation — but know that your starter may behave a touch differently than one fed on strong bread flour.

The best approach for beginners is a blend: start with whole grain flour for the first week to establish a robust culture, then transition to bread flour (or a mix of bread flour and 10-20% whole grain) for ongoing maintenance. You get the microbial diversity of whole grain with the structural predictability of refined flour.

## Water Quality

Water is the second half of the equation, and it deserves more attention than most beginners give it. Municipal tap water is treated with chlorine or chloramine to kill microorganisms — which is exactly what you are trying to cultivate. The irony is not lost on experienced bakers.

**Chlorine** evaporates relatively quickly. If your water smells faintly of swimming pools, simply fill a jug and leave it uncovered on the counter for an hour or two. The chlorine will off-gas, and the water becomes safe for your starter.

**Chloramine** is more persistent. Many modern water treatment plants use it because it does not evaporate. If you suspect chloramine (your water utility's website will confirm), you have a few options: use filtered water, use bottled spring water, or invest in a carbon filter that removes chloramine. Avoid distilled water — it lacks the minerals that support fermentation.

Water temperature at feeding time matters too. Lukewarm water (25-28 degrees Celsius) gives your starter a gentle boost. Cold water slows things down. Hot water above 40 degrees Celsius can kill yeast and bacteria outright. When in doubt, aim for water that feels neutral on the inside of your wrist — neither warm nor cool.

## Feeding Ratios

A feeding ratio describes the proportion of old starter to fresh flour to fresh water, expressed as starter : flour : water by weight. Understanding ratios gives you control over timing, acidity, and vigor.

**1:1:1** is the quickest ratio. Equal parts of everything. Your starter will peak in 3-4 hours at warm room temperature. This is useful when you need to build volume quickly — say, you need 300 grams of starter for a recipe and only have 50 grams. Feed it 1:1:1 several times in succession.

**1:2:2** is a moderate ratio and an excellent choice for pre-bake feedings. The starter peaks in roughly 4-6 hours, giving you a comfortable window to prepare your dough.

**1:5:5** is the standard maintenance ratio for a healthy, mature starter. A small amount of old culture inoculates a large amount of fresh food, resulting in a slower, more balanced fermentation over 8-12 hours. This is the ratio that fits neatly into a once-a-day feeding schedule and produces the most predictable, mild-flavored starter.

> **Weigh everything.** A kitchen scale accurate to 1 gram is the single most important tool in sourdough baking. Volume measurements (cups, tablespoons) are wildly inconsistent — a cup of flour can vary by 30% depending on how you scoop it. Precision matters here, and a digital scale costs less than a bag of specialty flour.

> **Deep Dive: Building Your Flour Library**
>
> Once your starter is alive and predictable, the real experimentation begins. Different flours produce different flavors, textures, and fermentation speeds. Building a small library of flours — even just three or four types — lets you tune your starter and your bread to your personal taste. Here is a detailed comparison of the flours most commonly used in sourdough baking.
>
> **Strong Bread Flour (11.5-13% protein)** is the workhorse. High protein means strong gluten, which means tall, open-crumbed loaves with excellent oven spring. Absorption is moderate — expect to use roughly 65-70% hydration for a standard dough. The flavor is clean and mildly wheaty, letting the sourdough character shine through. Brands vary significantly; look for unbleached, unbromated flour from mills that list protein content on the label. King Arthur Bread Flour (12.7%), Marriages Very Strong (14%), and local mill equivalents are all excellent choices.
>
> **All-Purpose Flour (9.5-11.5% protein)** produces a softer, more tender crumb. It absorbs less water than bread flour, so reduce hydration by 3-5% when substituting. Flavor is neutral. This flour excels in discard recipes — pancakes, crackers, waffles — where you want tenderness, not chew. For bread, it works but produces a denser loaf with less dramatic rise.
>
> **French T65 Tradition (11-12% protein)** is the flour behind classic French country bread. It is lightly sifted, retaining some of the outer bran layers that white flour discards. The result is a cream-colored crumb with subtle sweetness and extraordinary depth of flavor. Absorption is moderate to high. If you can source it, T65 makes stunning baguettes and batards. It ferments slightly faster than pure white flour due to the retained bran.
>
> **Italian Tipo 00 (8-12.5% protein)** is finely milled for a silky texture. Protein content varies widely by brand and intended use — pizza flour tipo 00 can be 12%+, while pastry tipo 00 may be under 9%. For sourdough bread, choose a high-protein tipo 00. The fine milling produces an exceptionally smooth dough that is a pleasure to work with. Absorption is lower; reduce hydration by 5-8%. The flavor is delicate, almost sweet.
>
> **Dark Rye Flour (8-10% protein)** is the fermentation accelerator. It is loaded with enzymes (amylase in particular) that break starches into sugars rapidly, giving wild yeast an aggressive food source. The flavor is deep, malty, and earthy. Dark rye absorbs significantly more water than wheat flour — up to 80-90% hydration is typical. Gluten is virtually absent in rye, so it contributes no structural strength. Use it at 10-20% of your total flour to add complexity and speed without losing the ability to shape a loaf.
>
> **Light Rye Flour (8-9% protein)** offers a milder version of dark rye's character. Less bran means less enzyme activity and a gentler flavor — pleasantly malty without the aggressive tang. It is easier to incorporate into wheat-based doughs. Many bakers add 5-10% light rye to their everyday bread formula for a whisper of complexity.
>
> **Whole Wheat Flour (13-14% protein)** is nutritionally dense and ferments fast. The bran and germ are rich in minerals, oils, and microbial life. Whole wheat absorbs considerably more water than white flour — increase hydration by 5-10%. The flavor is nutty, robust, and earthy. Used at 100%, it produces a dense, hearty loaf that ferments very quickly (watch your timing). Most bakers use 20-40% whole wheat blended with bread flour for the best balance of flavor and structure.
>
> **Spelt Flour (12-15% protein)** is an ancient wheat variety with a sweet, slightly nutty flavor. Despite its high protein content, spelt gluten is fragile and extensible rather than elastic. This means the dough stretches easily but tears if overworked. Reduce hydration by 5-10% compared to regular bread flour, handle gently, and shorten your bulk ferment. Spelt ferments fast because it is rich in sugars. The resulting bread has a delicate, almost cake-like crumb and a lovely golden crust.
>
> When building your flour library, start with three: a strong bread flour for daily feeding, a dark rye for boosting fermentation, and a whole wheat for depth of flavor. As you gain confidence, add T65 or spelt to explore new dimensions. Store all flour in airtight containers in a cool, dry place — whole grain flours in particular can go rancid if stored warm. Buy in quantities you will use within two months, and always note the protein content. That single number tells you more about how a flour will perform than any marketing on the package.

## Pro Tips

- **Start with rye, then transition.** Use dark rye or whole wheat for days 1-7 to establish a strong microbial culture, then switch to bread flour for daily maintenance. Your starter gets the best of both worlds.

- **Let tap water sit before using it.** Fill a jar or pitcher the night before and leave it uncovered. By morning, most chlorine has evaporated. This costs nothing and removes a common obstacle.

- **Buy a kitchen scale today, not tomorrow.** Volumetric measurements introduce chaos into a process that rewards consistency. A scale accurate to 1 gram costs under ten euros and will transform your baking.

- **Rotate your flours seasonally.** Whole wheat and rye in winter, when cooler temperatures slow fermentation. Lighter flours in summer, when heat accelerates everything. Match your ingredients to your environment.

- **Store whole grain flour in the fridge or freezer.** The oils in bran go rancid at room temperature within a few weeks. Cold storage extends shelf life to several months and preserves flavor.
