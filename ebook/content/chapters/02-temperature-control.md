# Temperature: Your Main Control

If there is one variable that separates frustrated beginners from confident bakers, it is temperature. Not flour. Not water. Not the brand of jar you keep your starter in. Temperature governs the speed of fermentation, the balance of acids in your culture, and the flavor of the bread that eventually comes out of your oven. Master temperature, and you master timing. Master timing, and sourdough fits into your life instead of the other way around.

The beautiful thing about temperature is that it is not an ingredient you need to buy. It is an invisible lever you can pull at any moment. Want your starter to peak faster so you can bake this afternoon? Warm it up. Want to slow everything down so you can bake tomorrow morning instead? Cool it down. Want a tangier loaf with more complexity? Let your dough ferment at a lower temperature for longer. Every decision about temperature is a decision about flavor and schedule, and once you internalize this, you will never feel at the mercy of your starter again.

Yet most beginners treat temperature as an afterthought. They feed their starter on the kitchen counter and hope for the best, not realizing that the counter next to the stove is five degrees warmer than the one by the window, or that their kitchen drops to 18 degrees Celsius overnight. This chapter will change how you see your kitchen — as a landscape of microclimates, each one a tool at your disposal.

---

## The Three Temperature Zones

Think of fermentation temperature in three broad zones, each producing different results.

**Warm (26-28 degrees Celsius)** is the sweet spot for vigorous, balanced fermentation. Wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria are both active and happy. Your starter will peak in a predictable window, the flavor will be mildly tangy with good sweetness, and the rise will be strong. This is the zone most bakers aim for during daily feedings and pre-bake preparations.

**Cool (20-22 degrees Celsius)** slows fermentation down significantly. Your starter will take longer to peak — perhaps 12-16 hours instead of 6-8. But something interesting happens at cooler temperatures: the balance of bacterial activity shifts. Lactic acid bacteria that produce **acetic acid** (the sharp, vinegary tang) become more dominant relative to those producing **lactic acid** (the mild, yogurt-like sourness). If you enjoy bread with a pronounced sour flavor, cooler fermentation is your path.

**Cold (3-5 degrees Celsius, the refrigerator)** nearly halts fermentation. Your starter will survive for a week or more in the fridge between feedings, making refrigeration the key to a low-maintenance schedule. Cold retarding your bread dough overnight also develops extraordinary flavor complexity. The slow, creeping fermentation in the fridge produces organic acids and aromatic compounds that fast, warm fermentation simply cannot match.

---

## Lactic Acid vs. Acetic Acid

The flavor of sourdough is shaped by two primary acids, and temperature determines which one dominates.

**Lactic acid** is smooth, round, and mild — think yogurt or buttermilk. Lactic acid bacteria thrive in warm, wet environments. A warm starter at 100% hydration will produce more lactic acid, resulting in bread that is pleasantly tangy without being sharp. This is the flavor profile most people associate with good sourdough.

**Acetic acid** is sharp, assertive, and vinegary — think of a San Francisco sourdough with real bite. Acetic acid-producing bacteria prefer cooler temperatures and stiffer (lower hydration) conditions. If you want more acetic character, lower your fermentation temperature to 20-22 degrees Celsius and consider reducing your starter's hydration to 60-80%.

Understanding this relationship gives you a flavor dial. Warm and wet for mild. Cool and stiff for tangy. Most bakers land somewhere in between, adjusting as the seasons change and their tastes evolve.

## Finding Warm Spots

Most kitchens have a spot that hovers around 26-28 degrees Celsius if you know where to look. Here are the most reliable options.

**On top of the refrigerator.** The compressor generates gentle, consistent heat. Place your starter jar on top of the fridge, and you may find it sits perfectly in the warm zone. Verify with a thermometer — some fridges run cooler than others.

**Near (not on) a radiator or heating vent.** Proximity is key. Directly on a radiator is far too hot; two feet away may be ideal. Experiment with distance until you find the sweet spot.

**Inside the oven with only the light on.** This is the classic sourdough trick. The incandescent bulb in most ovens generates enough warmth to raise the interior to 26-30 degrees Celsius. Place your starter inside, close the door, and leave only the light on. Put a sticky note on the oven controls — you do not want to preheat the oven and accidentally cook your starter.

> **Warning:** Always verify oven light temperature with a thermometer before relying on this method. Some LED oven lights produce almost no heat, while some older incandescent bulbs can push temperatures above 35 degrees Celsius. The danger zone begins at 35 degrees, where yeast starts to weaken, and by 40 degrees, you risk killing your culture entirely.

**A microwave (turned off) with a cup of hot water.** Place a mug of just-boiled water inside your microwave alongside your starter jar. Close the door. The enclosed space traps warmth and humidity. Replace the water every few hours as it cools. This works surprisingly well as a makeshift proofing box.

---

## The Danger Zone

Above 35 degrees Celsius, wild yeast begins to struggle. Activity slows, and unhelpful bacteria can gain a foothold. At 40-45 degrees, yeast cells start to die. If your starter has been exposed to sustained temperatures above 40 degrees — sitting in direct summer sun, left on a warm stovetop — it may need several feedings at proper temperature to recover, if it recovers at all.

Cold is far less dangerous. Your starter can survive weeks in the refrigerator and months in the freezer (though revival from frozen takes patience and multiple feedings). When in doubt, err on the side of too cool rather than too warm.

## The Thermometer Imperative

A simple digital probe thermometer is the second most important tool in sourdough baking, after a kitchen scale. You will use it to check water temperature before feeding, to measure the ambient temperature of your starter's resting spot, and to verify your dough temperature during mixing.

Instant-read digital thermometers are inexpensive and accurate. Some bakers also use small adhesive thermometer strips stuck to the outside of their starter jars for continuous ambient readings. Either approach works. The goal is to replace guesswork with data.

> **Deep Dive: The Temperature Map**
>
> Your kitchen is not one temperature. It is a mosaic of microclimates that shift throughout the day and across the seasons. Mapping these microclimates is one of the most valuable exercises you can do as a sourdough baker, because once you know where the warm spots and cool pockets are, you can place your starter and dough exactly where they need to be.
>
> **The Kitchen Temperature Audit**
>
> Set aside one day for this exercise. You will need a digital thermometer (probe or instant-read) and a notebook. Choose five locations in your kitchen and adjacent rooms where you might reasonably place a starter jar or a bowl of dough. Good candidates include: the countertop near the stove, the top of the refrigerator, inside the oven with the light on, a shelf in the pantry, and a spot near a window. At three different times — morning (7-8 AM), midday (12-1 PM), and evening (8-9 PM) — record the temperature at each spot. Write it down in a simple table.
>
> | Location | Morning | Midday | Evening |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Counter near stove | ___ C | ___ C | ___ C |
> | Top of fridge | ___ C | ___ C | ___ C |
> | Oven with light on | ___ C | ___ C | ___ C |
> | Pantry shelf | ___ C | ___ C | ___ C |
> | Near window | ___ C | ___ C | ___ C |
>
> You will be surprised by the variation. A kitchen that averages 22 degrees Celsius may have a spot on the fridge that sits at 27 degrees and a windowsill that drops to 18 degrees at night. That nine-degree range is the difference between a starter that peaks in six hours and one that takes fourteen.
>
> **Seasonal Adjustments**
>
> Repeat this audit once per season, or at least in summer and winter. Summer kitchens often run 25-30 degrees without any effort, which means your starter ferments faster and may over-proof if you are not paying attention. In summer, you may actually need to find cooler spots — the pantry, a north-facing room, or even the fridge for extended fermentation. Winter kitchens drop to 18-20 degrees, slowing everything down. This is when the oven light trick, the top of the fridge, and DIY proofing boxes become essential.
>
> **Building a DIY Proofing Box**
>
> A proofing box gives you temperature control regardless of your kitchen's ambient conditions. The concept is simple: an insulated enclosure with a gentle, controllable heat source.
>
> The easiest version uses a large plastic storage container (50-70 liters) with a lid. Place a reptile heating pad or a seedling heat mat underneath the container (not inside — you want indirect warmth). Set the mat to its lowest setting and place a thermometer inside the box to monitor temperature. Adjust by cracking the lid open if it runs too warm, or adding a towel over the lid for more insulation if it runs cool. Target 26-28 degrees Celsius inside the box.
>
> An even simpler approach uses a hot water bottle. Fill a standard rubber hot water bottle, wrap it in a towel, and place it inside a cooler bag or insulated box alongside your starter jar. The hot water bottle holds warmth for 4-6 hours before needing a refresh. This low-tech method works beautifully for overnight proofing in cold kitchens.
>
> For bakers who want precision without DIY, commercial proofing boxes (such as the Brod & Taylor folding proofer) offer thermostat-controlled warmth in a compact package. They are an investment, but they remove all temperature guesswork from the equation.
>
> **Reading Your Environment**
>
> Once you have mapped your kitchen and built (or bought) a proofing solution, the final step is learning to read environmental cues instinctively. On a warm summer evening when the kitchen holds at 27 degrees, you know your overnight dough will ferment faster — so you reduce the starter inoculation or use cooler water. On a cold January morning when the kitchen is 18 degrees, you know to move your starter to the warm spot on the fridge and extend your expected timeline by a few hours. This responsiveness to environment is what separates a baker who follows recipes from a baker who understands bread.

## Pro Tips

- **Always check water temperature before feeding.** It takes three seconds and prevents the two most common feeding mistakes: water too hot (kills yeast) and water too cold (stalls fermentation). Aim for 25-28 degrees Celsius.

- **Put a sticky note on the oven when your starter is inside.** This is not optional. It is the difference between a thriving culture and a small jar of cooked paste. Write "STARTER INSIDE" in large letters.

- **Use your environment, do not fight it.** A hot summer kitchen is an invitation to experiment with cold retards and refrigerator fermentation. A cold winter kitchen is perfect for long, slow, flavor-building ferments. Adapt your schedule to the season.

- **Keep a thermometer in your starter's resting spot permanently.** A small, inexpensive indoor thermometer placed next to your jar gives you a continuous read on conditions. You will start to correlate temperature with performance instinctively.

- **When in doubt, go cooler.** A slow, cool fermentation is always recoverable — you just wait longer. An overheated starter that has been damaged by excessive warmth may need days of careful feeding to bounce back.
