# Troubleshooting Guide

Something is wrong with your starter. Maybe it has stopped rising. Maybe it smells like a chemistry lab. Maybe there is a mysterious liquid floating on top, or a color you have never seen before, and your first instinct is to throw it out and start over. Do not. In the vast majority of cases, what looks like a catastrophe is actually a minor imbalance that can be corrected in one to three feedings.

The truth about sourdough starters is that they are extraordinarily difficult to kill. The organisms that live in your culture have evolved over thousands of years to survive harsh, acidic, nutrient-poor environments. A few days of neglect, a temperature swing, or an unfamiliar flour will not destroy them. It will stress them, and stressed organisms behave oddly -- but odd behavior is not death. It is a signal, and if you learn to read these signals, you can fix almost anything.

This chapter is your field guide to those signals. We will cover every common symptom you are likely to encounter, from the mildly puzzling to the genuinely alarming, and walk through the diagnosis and fix for each one. Keep this chapter bookmarked. You will come back to it.

## The Basics: What "Healthy" Looks Like

Before you can diagnose a problem, you need to know what normal looks like. A healthy, mature starter fed at a 1:5:5 ratio at room temperature should rise to roughly double its original volume within six to twelve hours, have a domed top at peak (slightly rounded, not flat or sunken), smell pleasantly tangy and yeasty -- like yogurt and fresh bread, have a bubbly, mousse-like texture throughout, and pass the float test at or near peak.

Not every feeding will be identical. A starter might peak at eight hours one day and ten the next. That is normal variation. What matters is the overall pattern: consistent rising, consistent aroma, consistent texture. When that pattern breaks, that is when you start troubleshooting.

## Common Symptoms and Fixes

**Hooch (liquid on top).** That dark liquid sitting on top of your starter is hooch -- a mix of alcohol, water, and organic acids produced by fermentation. It appears when the starter has consumed all its available food and the microbes are essentially sending up a distress flare. It is harmless. You can pour it off or stir it back in (stirring it in will increase sourness). Then feed immediately, and consider increasing your feeding frequency or your flour-to-starter ratio.

**Acetone or nail polish remover smell.** This sharp, chemical odor is acetic acid production running unchecked, usually because the starter has gone too long without food. The acetic acid-producing bacteria dominate when the culture is stressed and starving. The fix is straightforward: discard all but a small amount (a tablespoon), feed with fresh flour and water, and resume regular feedings. The smell should clear within two to three feedings.

**Bubbles but no rise.** Your starter has bubbles throughout, which means fermentation is happening, but it is not actually rising in the jar. This usually means the gluten network is too weak to trap the gas. Common causes: too-high hydration, low-protein flour, or excessive acidity breaking down what little gluten has formed. Try feeding with bread flour instead of all-purpose, and reduce hydration slightly (use equal weights of flour and water rather than a thinner consistency).

**Sluggish or no activity.** The starter sits there doing nothing. No bubbles, no rise, no smell beyond raw flour. If the starter is new (less than two weeks old), this is likely just the immature culture still establishing itself. Keep feeding consistently. If it is a mature starter that has suddenly stopped, check the temperature -- cold kitchens slow fermentation dramatically. Move it somewhere warmer (25-28 degrees) and feed with warm water. Activity should return within one to two feedings.

**Strange smells in the first week.** During the first seven to ten days of creating a new starter, you will encounter some truly unpleasant smells: vomit, cheese, dirty socks, wet dog. This is normal and expected. The initial days are dominated by leuconostoc and other bacteria that produce foul-smelling compounds. These populations are temporary -- they cannot survive the increasing acidity. By day seven to ten, the lactic acid bacteria take over and the smell transitions to a clean, tangy sourness. Do not give up during this phase. It passes.

**Mold.** Actual mold -- fuzzy spots of white, green, black, or pink on the surface -- is the one genuinely serious problem. Mold is rare in a healthy, acidic starter because the low pH inhibits mold growth. If you see it, the culture's pH has been too high, usually because of infrequent feeding or contamination from dirty equipment. Small spots of white mold on the surface can sometimes be scraped off, with the remaining starter fed aggressively for several days. But green, black, or pink mold means you should discard the entire culture and start fresh. Sterilize the jar thoroughly before beginning again.

## Consistency Over Perfection

The single most common cause of starter problems is inconsistent feeding. Not bad flour, not bad water, not the wrong jar -- just an erratic schedule. Feeding at 8 AM one day, 11 PM the next, skipping a day, then feeding twice. The microbial community responds to rhythm. When the rhythm is chaotic, the community becomes unstable, and unstable communities produce unpredictable results.

You do not need laboratory precision. But picking a rough time of day, using a consistent ratio, and sticking to that routine will solve more problems than any troubleshooting guide ever could.

> **Deep Dive: The Diagnosis Flowchart**
>
> When something goes wrong, the fastest path to a fix is systematic diagnosis. Below are the most common symptoms you will encounter, each mapped to its likely causes and specific solutions. Work through them one at a time. Most problems resolve within two to four feedings once you identify the cause.
>
> **Symptom: Starter will not rise at all**
> Possible causes: Kitchen too cold (below 18 degrees), starter too young (under two weeks old), dead or severely weakened culture from extended neglect.
> Solutions: Move to a warm spot (25-28 degrees). Use warm water (28-30 degrees) for the next two feedings. If the starter is new, continue feeding consistently for at least fourteen days before worrying. If the starter has been neglected for months, discard all but a teaspoon, feed at 1:2:2 with bread flour, and repeat every twelve hours for three to four days.
>
> **Symptom: Rose and then stopped rising**
> Possible causes: False start from leuconostoc bacteria (common on days 2-4 of a new starter), or ambient temperature dropped significantly.
> Solutions: If the starter is new, this is the classic "day three crash." The initial rise was from leuconostoc, not from established sourdough organisms. Keep feeding through it -- genuine yeast and LAB activity usually begins around days 5-7. If the starter is mature, check for temperature changes in your kitchen.
>
> **Symptom: Rises, but very slowly**
> Possible causes: Cool kitchen temperature, low-activity flour (bleached or old flour), feeding ratio too high for conditions.
> Solutions: Increase temperature (warm water, warm spot). Switch to unbleached bread flour. Reduce ratio to 1:2:2 or 1:3:3 to concentrate the microbial population. Feed with slightly warm water (28 degrees).
>
> **Symptom: Too sour (sharp, vinegary)**
> Possible causes: Excess acetic acid from long fermentation, cool temperatures favoring heterofermentative bacteria, infrequent feeding.
> Solutions: Feed more frequently. Use warmer temperatures (26-28 degrees), which favor lactic acid (smooth sourness) over acetic acid (sharp sourness). Increase the feeding ratio to give the culture more food and shorten the time it spends in the acidic decline phase.
>
> **Symptom: Not sour enough (bland)**
> Possible causes: Feeding too frequently, warm temperatures favoring yeast over bacteria, high feeding ratio diluting the culture too aggressively.
> Solutions: Let the starter ferment longer before feeding. Use cooler temperatures (20-22 degrees). Add a small proportion of whole wheat or rye flour (10-20%) to boost bacterial activity. Try a longer, cooler bulk fermentation in your bread dough.
>
> **Symptom: Grey or dark color on top**
> Possible causes: Oxidation from exposure to air, hooch forming and mixing with flour pigments, completely normal in a neglected starter.
> Solutions: This is almost always cosmetic and harmless. Stir it in or pour off the dark liquid, discard most of the starter, and feed. Color should return to normal within one to two feedings.
>
> **Symptom: Pink or orange streaks**
> Possible causes: Contamination by *Serratia marcescens* or other pigmented bacteria, sometimes from contaminated flour or unsanitized equipment.
> Solutions: Discard the entire starter. Do not attempt to save it. Pink and orange colorations indicate organisms that should not be present in a healthy culture. Sterilize your jar and utensils, use fresh flour from a different source, and start a new culture.
>
> **Symptom: Hooch appears every day (or after every feeding)**
> Possible causes: Starter is consistently running out of food before the next feeding. Ratio too low, feedings too infrequent, or kitchen too warm.
> Solutions: Increase the feeding ratio (try 1:8:8 or 1:10:10). Feed more frequently if at room temperature. If you cannot feed twice daily, move the starter to the fridge between feedings.
>
> **Symptom: Liquid layer appears immediately after feeding**
> Possible causes: Flour not fully hydrated, or the starter's consistency is too thin.
> Solutions: Stir more thoroughly at feeding time to ensure complete hydration. If using a high-hydration ratio, reduce the water slightly until the consistency is like thick pancake batter rather than a pourable liquid.
>
> **Symptom: Smells like cheese (strong, pungent)**
> Possible causes: Butyric acid production, common in young starters (first week) or in starters that have been neglected. Certain bacteria produce butyric acid when the culture is out of balance.
> Solutions: If the starter is new, keep feeding -- this phase passes as lactic acid bacteria establish dominance. If the starter is mature, it has likely been underfed. Discard aggressively (keep only a teaspoon), feed at 1:5:5, and repeat every twelve hours until the smell normalizes.
>
> **Symptom: Smells like alcohol (boozy, wine-like)**
> Possible causes: Yeast producing ethanol after consuming available sugars, usually because the starter has gone past peak and is in the decline phase. Common and harmless.
> Solutions: Feed sooner. Catch the starter closer to peak rather than letting it collapse fully. If the smell is persistent and strong, increase feeding frequency or ratio.
>
> **Symptom: Flat and pancake-like (spreads, no dome)**
> Possible causes: Over-fermented (passed peak and collapsed), weak gluten structure from excessive acidity, or very high hydration.
> Solutions: Feed more frequently to prevent over-fermentation. Use bread flour for stronger gluten. Reduce hydration slightly. Mark the post-feeding level on your jar so you can see when it peaks and catch it before it collapses.
>
> **Symptom: Separating into layers (dense bottom, liquid top)**
> Possible causes: Severely underfed. The culture has completely exhausted its food and the heavier flour particles have settled while the liquid byproducts rise to the top.
> Solutions: Pour off the liquid, stir the remaining paste, discard all but a tablespoon, and feed with fresh flour and water. Resume regular feedings. This is a neglect issue, not a health issue -- the organisms are still alive.
>
> **Symptom: Slow to peak (takes 16+ hours)**
> Possible causes: Cold kitchen, low starter-to-flour ratio, weak or immature culture, old or low-quality flour.
> Solutions: Increase temperature. Reduce ratio to 1:2:2 to concentrate the culture. Switch to fresh, unbleached bread flour. Give the starter two to three days of consistent twice-daily feedings to rebuild strength.
>
> **Symptom: Inconsistent behavior day to day**
> Possible causes: Fluctuating kitchen temperature (e.g., warm during the day, cold overnight), inconsistent feeding schedule, switching between different flour types.
> Solutions: Standardize your feeding time and ratio. Use the same flour consistently. Find a spot in your kitchen with stable temperature. Once you establish a routine, the starter will stabilize within three to five feedings. Consistency is the single most powerful troubleshooting tool you have.
>
> **When to start over.** In all the scenarios above, only two genuinely warrant discarding and starting fresh: visible mold (green, black, or pink fuzzy growth) and persistent pink or orange discoloration. Everything else -- hooch, bad smells, sluggish activity, weird colors, strange textures -- can be resolved through patient, consistent feeding. Your starter wants to survive. Give it a reason to.

## Pro Tips

- **Use your nose first.** Smell is the fastest, most reliable diagnostic tool for a sourdough starter. A healthy starter smells like yogurt and yeast. Sharp vinegar means it is hungry. Acetone means it is starving. Cheese or vomit in the first week is normal. Rotten or putrid after the first week is not. Train your nose and it will tell you what the starter needs before any visual cue does.

- **The jar tells a story.** Dried residue on the inside of your jar shows how high the starter climbed before it fell. If the residue line is high and the current level is low, your starter peaked and collapsed while you were not watching -- you are feeding too late. If there is no residue above the current level, it has not risen yet -- you are checking too early, or it needs help.

- **Change one variable at a time.** When troubleshooting, resist the urge to change the flour, the ratio, the water temperature, and the feeding schedule all at once. Change one thing, observe for two to three feedings, then adjust further if needed. If you change everything simultaneously, you will not know what fixed the problem -- or what made it worse.

- **Filtered water is rarely necessary.** Municipal tap water is fine for the vast majority of starters. Chloramine (used in most modern water treatment) dissipates quickly and does not meaningfully affect fermentation. If you are genuinely concerned, let the water sit uncovered for an hour before using it. Bottled water is almost never needed and adds unnecessary cost.

- **When in doubt, feed it.** The answer to almost every sourdough starter problem is the same: discard, feed, wait, repeat. Patience and consistency fix more issues than any exotic technique, special flour, or internet hack. Feed your starter, give it warmth, give it time, and it will come back.
