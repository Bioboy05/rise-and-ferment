# Hydration Explained

There is a moment, early in every baker's journey, when someone mentions hydration percentage and the room goes quiet. It sounds technical. Mathematical. Intimidating. But hydration is simply the ratio of water to flour in your starter (or dough), expressed as a percentage. If you use equal weights of flour and water, you have 100% hydration. If you use less water than flour, the number drops. That is the entire concept. Once it clicks, you gain control over your starter's consistency, its flavor, how fast it ferments, and what kind of bread it produces.

Most beginners start at 100% hydration — equal parts flour and water by weight — because it is the simplest ratio to remember and produces a starter with a pancake-batter consistency that is easy to stir and observe. And for many bakers, 100% hydration is where they stay, happily, for years. But if you have ever wondered why some bakeries produce bread with a sharp, assertive tang while others make loaves that are mellow and sweet, hydration is part of the answer. Paired with temperature, it is the second great lever you can pull to shape your sourdough's character.

This chapter will take the mystery out of hydration. You will learn the formula, understand what different hydration levels look and feel like, and discover why a stiffer or wetter starter might suit your baking style. By the end, you will be able to look at a recipe that calls for a "stiff levain" or a "liquid starter" and know exactly what to do.

---

## The Hydration Formula

Hydration percentage is calculated as:

**Hydration % = (weight of water / weight of flour) x 100**

If you feed your starter 50 grams of flour and 50 grams of water, the hydration is (50 / 50) x 100 = 100%. If you feed it 50 grams of flour and 40 grams of water, the hydration is (40 / 50) x 100 = 80%. The formula only considers the fresh flour and water you add at each feeding — the existing starter's hydration is already set from its previous feeding.

This percentage tells you how wet or stiff your starter will be, which in turn affects fermentation speed, acid production, and how the starter behaves when you use it in a recipe.

---

## What Different Hydration Levels Feel Like

**100% hydration** is the standard. Your starter has the consistency of thick pancake batter. It pours slowly, holds its shape briefly, and shows vigorous bubbling on the surface as it ferments. Stir it with a spoon, and it flows back together smoothly. This is the most common maintenance hydration because it is easy to mix, easy to observe, and works beautifully in most bread recipes.

**80% hydration** is thicker — more like a very dense batter or a soft, sticky dough. It does not pour; you scoop it. Bubbles are still visible on the surface but the overall texture is denser. At 80% hydration, fermentation slows slightly compared to 100%, and the balance begins to shift toward acetic acid production. Some bakers prefer this consistency because it is less messy and slightly more predictable in timing.

**60% hydration** produces a stiff, dough-like starter. It does not flow at all. You mix it with your hands or a stiff spoon, kneading the flour and water together into a firm ball. This is a "stiff starter" or "stiff levain." It ferments slowly — 10-16 hours at room temperature is typical — and produces noticeably more acetic acid, giving your bread a sharper tang. Italian biga and French levain dur are built on this concept. The stiff consistency also makes the starter easy to store: it does not stick to everything it touches.

**Higher hydrations (110-125%)** are less common but used by some professional bakers. These very liquid starters ferment fast and develop strong lactic acid character — mild, creamy, yogurt-like sourness. They are harder to manage at home because they flow everywhere, stick to jars, and can over-ferment quickly in warm kitchens.

## How Hydration Affects Flavor

The connection between hydration and flavor is one of the most powerful tools in a baker's arsenal. The science is straightforward: certain lactic acid bacteria prefer wetter environments, while others prefer drier conditions. Bacteria that produce **lactic acid** (smooth, mild sourness) thrive in high-hydration, warm starters. Bacteria that produce **acetic acid** (sharp, vinegary tang) prefer low-hydration, cooler starters.

This means you can dial flavor in two directions at once. A warm, wet starter (100% hydration at 26-28 degrees Celsius) gives you mellow, balanced bread. A cool, stiff starter (60% hydration at 20-22 degrees Celsius) gives you assertive, tangy bread. Most bakers land somewhere in the middle, but knowing the extremes lets you move intentionally toward the flavor profile you want.

> **For beginners:** Start at 100% hydration and stay there until your starter is mature, predictable, and doubling reliably. Experimenting with hydration is rewarding, but only after you have established a stable baseline. Give yourself at least three to four weeks of consistent feedings before branching out.

## Hydration and Bread Recipes

When a bread recipe calls for a specific amount of starter, the hydration of that starter affects the overall hydration of your dough. A recipe designed for 100% hydration starter assumes that the starter contributes equal parts flour and water. If you use a 60% hydration starter instead, you are adding more flour and less water than the recipe expects, which will make your dough drier and stiffer.

Experienced bakers adjust for this. If you maintain a stiff starter and the recipe assumes a liquid one, you either convert the starter to 100% hydration with a pre-bake feeding, or you recalculate the flour and water in the recipe to compensate. For most home bakers, the simplest approach is to maintain your starter at whatever hydration you prefer and do a single pre-bake feeding at the hydration the recipe specifies.

> **Deep Dive: The Hydration Ladder**
>
> This is a practical experiment designed to teach your hands and eyes what different hydration levels look, feel, and taste like. Set aside a day for it. You will create five small test starters at different hydrations, observe them side by side, and record your findings. By the end, you will have an intuitive feel for hydration that no amount of reading can provide.
>
> **Preparation:** You will need five small jars or glasses (250 ml each is fine), a kitchen scale, a marker for labeling, your established starter as the seed culture, and your regular feeding flour. Label each jar with its target hydration: 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 100%.
>
> **60% Hydration — The Firm Ball**
> Mix 10 grams of your active starter with 50 grams of flour and 30 grams of water (30/50 = 60%). Stir until combined, then knead briefly with your fingers. The result is a firm, dry ball of dough. It holds its shape completely. Press your thumb into it and the indentation stays. Place it in the jar and mark the starting height with your marker. At 25 degrees Celsius, expect it to peak in 10-14 hours. When it does peak, the ball will have expanded, showing cracks on the surface where gas has pushed outward. The aroma will be mildly sharp, almost wine-like. This is the classic stiff levain — ideal for breads where you want assertive tang and a long, slow fermentation.
>
> **70% Hydration — The Dense Paste**
> Mix 10 grams of starter with 50 grams of flour and 35 grams of water. This produces a thick, heavy paste that resists stirring. It does not flow. You need to press and fold it together. In the jar, it slumps slowly into a flat-topped mass. Expect a peak in 8-12 hours. The rise is modest but visible — gas bubbles trapped inside push the surface upward unevenly. The aroma is more balanced than 60%, with less acetic sharpness and the beginning of creamy lactic character. This hydration is less common but some bakers find it to be a perfect middle ground for maintaining a starter that leans tangy without being as dense as a full stiff levain.
>
> **80% Hydration — The Thick Batter**
> Mix 10 grams of starter with 50 grams of flour and 40 grams of water. Now you have something that stirs with a spoon, though it takes effort. The consistency is like thick oatmeal or heavy hummus. It sits in the jar with a domed top and shows bubbles breaking through the surface as fermentation progresses. Peak in 6-10 hours. The dome is pronounced, and the aroma balances lactic and acetic beautifully — tangy but rounded, complex without aggression. Many professional bakeries maintain their starters at roughly this hydration for its balance of manageability, timing, and flavor.
>
> **90% Hydration — The Flowing Batter**
> Mix 10 grams of starter with 50 grams of flour and 45 grams of water. This pours slowly, like thick yogurt. Stir it with a spoon and it reluctantly flows back together, leaving a trail. Fermentation is visible as a bubbly, frothy surface. Peak in 5-8 hours. The aroma leans lactic — mild, creamy, pleasantly sour without any vinegar bite. The rise is dramatic because gas moves easily through the loose structure. This hydration is forgiving for beginners because the active fermentation is so visible and easy to read.
>
> **100% Hydration — The Pancake Batter**
> Mix 10 grams of starter with 50 grams of flour and 50 grams of water. Smooth, pourable, the consistency of thick pancake batter. It levels itself flat in the jar. Fermentation produces a foamy, bubbly surface that rises significantly — a healthy 100% hydration starter can double or even triple in volume. Peak in 4-8 hours depending on temperature. The aroma is mild, sweet-sour, yogurt-like. This is the most commonly recommended maintenance hydration because it is simple, predictable, and visually communicative. You can read the state of your starter at a glance.
>

You will know which hydration suits your taste, your schedule, and your baking goals. Many bakers discover through this experiment that their ideal maintenance hydration is not 100% after all, but 75% or 80% — a consistency that fits their kitchen, their timing, and the flavor they love.

## Pro Tips

- **Match your hydration to your schedule.** A stiff starter (60-70%) ferments slowly and gives you a wider window before it over-proofs. A wet starter (100%) ferments faster and peaks sooner. Choose the hydration that aligns with when you want to bake.

- **Use hydration to control tang.** If your bread is too mild, drop your starter's hydration to 70-80% and ferment at a cooler temperature. If it is too sharp, raise hydration to 100% and keep things warm. You are adjusting the same dial from both ends.

- **When switching hydration, give it two feedings to stabilize.** The microbial population shifts when you change conditions. Feed at the new hydration twice before using the starter in a bake, so the culture has time to rebalance.

- **Stiff starters travel well.** If you are going on vacation and want to bring your starter along (or share it with a friend), convert it to 60% hydration. It will survive longer between feedings and is far less messy to transport than a jar of liquid batter.

- **Remember the formula.** Water divided by flour, times 100. Write it on a sticky note inside your flour cabinet. Within a week, you will not need it anymore — but having it there removes the last excuse for guessing.
