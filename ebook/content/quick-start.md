# Quick-Start Checklist

Everything you need to go from zero to your first loaf — no fluff, no filler. Print this out and tape it to your fridge.

---

## Essential Equipment

### Must-Haves

**Kitchen scale (digital)**
Sourdough baking is all about ratios. Volume measurements (cups, tablespoons) are wildly inconsistent — a cup of flour can vary by 30% depending on how you scoop. A basic digital scale that reads in grams will cost you less than a bag of premium flour, and it will transform your results overnight.
- Budget option: Any scale accurate to 1g (around $10-15)

**Clear glass jar (1 liter / quart)**
You need to see what your starter is doing. A tall, straight-sided jar lets you observe bubbles, track rise, and spot the peak. Wide-mouth jars are easiest to stir and clean.
- Budget option: A cleaned pasta sauce jar works perfectly. Mark the level with a rubber band after feeding.

**Rubber band or dry-erase marker**
Place the band at the starter's level right after feeding. This is how you measure rise — without it, you are guessing. A strip of tape and a pen work just as well.

**Large mixing bowl**
A wide, shallow bowl (4-5 liter capacity) for mixing and bulk fermenting your dough. Glass or stainless steel are ideal because they do not retain odors and are easy to clean.

**Bench scraper**
A flat, rectangular metal or plastic tool for handling dough, dividing it, and scraping your work surface clean. Once you use one, you will wonder how you ever baked without it.
- Budget option: A stiff plastic gift card in a pinch, though a real bench scraper costs only a few dollars

**Dutch oven or heavy pot with lid**
This is the game-changer for home bakers. The sealed environment traps steam during the first phase of baking, mimicking a professional steam-injected oven. The result: dramatically better oven spring and a shatteringly crisp crust.
- Budget option: Any oven-safe pot with a tight lid (even a stainless steel stockpot) works. You can also use an inverted roasting pan as a lid over a baking sheet.

**Bread lame or sharp razor blade**
For scoring your dough before it goes in the oven. A lame (pronounced "LAHM") holds a razor at the right angle for clean, confident cuts. Scoring controls how your bread expands and prevents random blowouts.
- Budget option: A fresh, unwrapped razor blade held carefully at a 30-degree angle. A very sharp serrated knife can also work.

### Nice-to-Haves

**Banneton / proofing basket**
Rattan baskets that support your shaped dough during the final proof and leave beautiful spiral flour rings on the crust. They come in round and oval shapes.
- Budget option: A bowl lined with a well-floured linen or cotton tea towel. Use rice flour for dusting to prevent sticking.

**Kitchen thermometer (instant-read)**
Temperature affects everything in sourdough. Knowing your dough temperature, your water temperature, and your kitchen temperature removes a huge amount of guesswork.
- Budget option: Your hand. If water feels comfortably warm on your wrist (not hot), it is roughly 28-32C (82-90F).

**Parchment paper**
For transferring proofed dough into a scorching hot Dutch oven without burning your hands or deflating the loaf. Silicone-coated parchment is reusable for a few bakes.

**Spray bottle**
A quick spritz of water on the dough before closing the Dutch oven lid adds extra steam for better crust development. Not essential, but a nice touch.

**Wire cooling rack**
Bread continues cooking internally after leaving the oven. Cooling on a rack allows air to circulate underneath, preventing a soggy bottom.

> **Pro tip:** Start with the must-haves only. You can bake exceptional bread with a scale, a jar, a bowl, a bench scraper, and a Dutch oven. Everything else is refinement.

## Your First 7 Days

A day-by-day quick reference for creating a starter from scratch. Each day takes about 5 minutes of hands-on work.

> **Before you begin:** Choose a flour. Whole wheat or rye flour works best for the first few days because they contain more wild yeast and nutrients than white flour. You can transition to all-purpose later.

---

### Day 1 — The Beginning

**What to do:**
- Mix 50g whole wheat (or rye) flour + 50g lukewarm water (around 28C / 82F) in your clean jar
- Stir vigorously until smooth and no dry flour remains
- Mark the level with a rubber band
- Cover loosely (cloth, paper towel, or lid set on top without sealing)
- Place somewhere warm (24-28C / 75-82F)

**What to expect:**
Not much. Maybe a few small bubbles by evening. It may smell like wet flour or slightly musty. This is completely normal.

**Troubleshooting:**
If you see zero activity, do not worry. Some starters take 48 hours before anything visible happens. Patience is your most important ingredient today.

---

### Day 2 — Waiting

**What to do:**
- Look for any bubbles or rise. Do not feed yet — let the initial microbes establish themselves
- If you see some bubbles, great. If not, wait until Day 3
- Keep the jar in its warm spot

**What to expect:**
You may see a few bubbles forming on the surface or along the sides of the jar. There may be a slight tangy or yeasty smell developing. Some starters show dramatic bubbling on Day 2 — this is bacteria, not yeast. Do not get too excited yet.

**Troubleshooting:**
A faintly unpleasant smell (cheesy, funky) is normal at this stage. The bacterial population is establishing itself before the yeast takes over. Trust the process.

---

### Day 3 — First Feeding

**What to do:**
- Discard all but about 50g of your starter (roughly half the jar)
- Add 50g flour + 50g lukewarm water
- Stir vigorously, mark the new level, cover loosely
- Return to the warm spot

**What to expect:**
The starter may have risen, or it may look flat with bubbles. It might smell sour, funky, or like overripe fruit. All normal. After feeding, it will look smooth and thick.

**Troubleshooting:**
The dramatic rise you may have seen on Day 2 often disappears now. This is the infamous "Day 3 slump" — the initial bacteria die off and the real sourdough microbes start colonizing. It is not a setback. It is progress.

---

### Day 4 — Building Rhythm

**What to do:**
- Discard down to 50g
- Feed with 50g flour + 50g lukewarm water
- Stir, mark, cover, warm spot — same routine

**What to expect:**
You may see some bubbles but minimal rise. The smell should be shifting from funky toward something more pleasantly sour or tangy. The texture might be slightly looser than yesterday.

**Troubleshooting:**
If it smells strongly of acetone (nail polish remover), your starter is very hungry. This is not dangerous — it just means fermentation is active and the microbes are consuming food quickly. Feed on schedule and the smell will mellow.

---

### Day 5 — Signs of Life

**What to do:**
- Discard down to 50g
- Feed with 50g flour + 50g lukewarm water
- You can start transitioning to all-purpose flour if you like (try a 50/50 blend)
- Same routine: stir, mark, cover, warm spot

**What to expect:**
More consistent bubbling. You may see the starter rise 25-50% before falling. The aroma should be turning more pleasant — tangy, slightly fruity, bread-like. The texture becomes more elastic and stretchy when you stir.

**Troubleshooting:**
If your starter still shows no bubbles by Day 5, check your water — heavily chlorinated tap water can inhibit fermentation. Try filtered water or leave tap water uncovered overnight to let chlorine evaporate.

---

### Day 6 — Gaining Strength

**What to do:**
- Discard down to 50g
- Feed with 50g flour + 50g lukewarm water
- Start feeding twice daily if the starter is rising and falling within 6-8 hours (morning and evening)

**What to expect:**
The starter should be visibly bubbly and rising predictably. You will start to see a pattern: it rises, peaks, then falls. The timing of this cycle tells you how active it is. The aroma should be pleasantly sour and yeasty.

**Troubleshooting:**
If it rises quickly (within 3-4 hours) and then collapses, it is hungry. Increase the feeding ratio: try 25g starter + 50g flour + 50g water to give it more food to work through.

---

### Day 7 — Ready to Test

**What to do:**
- Feed as usual
- When the starter is at its peak (doubled in size, domed on top, lots of bubbles), try the float test: drop a small spoonful into a glass of water
- If it floats, your starter is likely strong enough for its first bake
- If it sinks, keep feeding for another 2-3 days — it needs more time

**What to expect:**
A healthy Day 7 starter doubles in size within 4-8 hours of feeding, has a network of bubbles throughout (not just on the surface), and smells pleasantly tangy and yeasty. It should feel airy and stretchy when you stir it.

**Troubleshooting:**
Many starters are not fully ready at Day 7 — and that is perfectly fine. Some take 10-14 days to become reliably strong. Keep feeding consistently, keep the environment warm, and it will get there. No two starters develop on exactly the same timeline.

> **Remember:** A mature starter doubles predictably, has a tangy-sweet aroma, and passes the float test consistently. If yours is not there yet, every day of feeding makes it stronger. You are building a living colony — it takes the time it takes.
