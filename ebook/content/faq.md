# Frequently Asked Questions

The 20 questions every sourdough beginner asks — answered clearly, honestly, and without judgment. We have all been there.

---

## Getting Started

### Q: Can I use all-purpose flour to make a starter?

Yes, absolutely. All-purpose flour works well for maintaining a starter once it is established. That said, whole wheat or rye flour is better for the first 5-7 days because they carry more wild yeast and nutrients that help kickstart fermentation. Many bakers use a blend: whole wheat for the first week, then transition to all-purpose for daily maintenance. Use whatever you have — your starter will adapt.

### Q: How long until my starter is ready to bake with?

Most starters become reliably active between 7 and 14 days. Some take longer, especially in cooler kitchens or with heavily processed flour. You will know it is ready when it consistently doubles in size within 4-8 hours of feeding and passes the float test. Do not rush it — a weak starter produces dense, flat bread. A few extra days of patience will save you from a disappointing first bake.

### Q: Do I need special water?

You need water that is not heavily chlorinated. Most municipal tap water is fine — let it sit uncovered for an hour to allow chlorine to evaporate, or use a basic filter. Avoid distilled water (it lacks minerals the microbes benefit from) and do not use water that has gone through a water softener. If your tap water tastes fine to drink, it will almost certainly work for sourdough.

### Q: Can I skip discarding starter when I feed it?

You can, but you probably should not — at least not regularly. Without discarding, you would need to double the flour and water each feeding, which gets expensive and unwieldy fast (by Day 7 you would have over 6 kilograms of starter). More importantly, discarding keeps the right balance of microbes. The good news: "discard" is not waste. Use it in pancakes, waffles, crackers, pizza dough, or flatbreads.

### Q: What if I forget to feed my starter for a day?

One day will not kill it. Your starter might develop hooch (a dark liquid layer on top) and smell strongly of alcohol or acetone, but it is almost certainly still alive. Pour off the hooch, discard as usual, feed with fresh flour and water, and give it 2-3 feedings to bounce back. Sourdough starters are remarkably resilient — they have survived for centuries precisely because they can handle neglect.

## Feeding & Maintenance

### Q: How often do I feed my starter?

It depends on where you keep it. At room temperature, feed once or twice daily. In the refrigerator, feed once a week. If you bake frequently (2-3 times per week), keep it at room temperature and feed daily. If you bake once a week or less, refrigerate it and pull it out the day before you plan to bake, giving it 2-3 feedings to wake up.

### Q: Can I change the type of flour I feed my starter?

Yes, and your starter will adapt. Switching flour types (say, from all-purpose to bread flour, or adding some rye) may cause a day or two of sluggish behavior while the microbial community adjusts, but it will normalize. Many bakers experiment with different flours to change flavor profiles. Just make any transitions gradually if possible — a 50/50 blend for a feeding or two smooths the shift.

### Q: How do I know my starter is ready to bake with?

Look for three signs: it has doubled (or nearly doubled) in size since its last feeding, the surface is domed and covered in bubbles, and it smells pleasantly tangy and yeasty — not harsh or like nail polish. The float test (dropping a spoonful into water) is a helpful indicator but not foolproof. The most reliable test is consistent predictability — if it doubles at roughly the same speed after each feeding, it is strong enough.

### Q: Can I have more than one starter?

Absolutely. Many bakers maintain multiple starters fed with different flours — a white starter, a whole wheat starter, a rye starter — because each produces distinct flavors. The practical constraint is maintenance: each one needs regular feeding. If you are starting out, master one starter first. Once it is stable and you are comfortable with the rhythm, experiment with a second.

### Q: How long can my starter survive in the fridge?

A well-fed, mature starter can easily survive 1-2 weeks in the refrigerator without attention. Many bakers have revived starters after a month or even longer of cold neglect. Before refrigerating, give it a fresh feeding and let it start to bubble (about 1-2 hours), then cap it loosely and refrigerate. When you are ready to use it again, pull it out, discard, and give it 2-3 feedings at room temperature until it is reliably doubling.

## Baking

### Q: Why is my bread flat?

The three most common causes are: under-developed starter (it was not at peak activity when you mixed the dough), over-fermentation (you let the dough go too long during bulk and it lost its structure), or weak shaping (not enough surface tension to hold the shape during baking). Start by ensuring your starter is at its absolute peak, watch your dough carefully during bulk fermentation, and practice building tight surface tension during shaping.

### Q: Why is my bread too sour (or not sour enough)?

Sourness comes from acid production during fermentation. More time = more sour. Cold temperatures favor acetic acid (vinegar-like sharpness), while warm temperatures favor lactic acid (creamy tang). To reduce sourness: shorter bulk fermentation, warmer temperatures, and feed your starter more frequently before baking. To increase sourness: longer cold retard in the refrigerator (24-48 hours), less starter in the dough, and incorporate some whole grain flour.

### Q: Can I bake sourdough without a Dutch oven?

Yes, though a Dutch oven makes it significantly easier. The key function of a Dutch oven is trapping steam around the bread during the first 15-20 minutes. Without one, you can: place a metal pan of boiling water on the bottom rack, spray water into the oven when you load the bread, or bake on a preheated baking stone/steel and cover with an inverted roasting pan. The crust will not be quite as dramatic, but you can still bake excellent bread.

### Q: How long should I let bread cool before cutting?

At least one hour, ideally two. This is harder than it sounds — the smell is incredible. But your bread is still cooking internally when it comes out of the oven. Cutting too early releases steam that should be reabsorbed, leaving you with a gummy, underdone crumb. The crust will also soften as it cools and then crisp up again. If you absolutely cannot wait, pull off a piece from the end rather than slicing through the middle.

### Q: Why didn't I get an ear on my bread?

An ear forms when one side of the score is undercut at a shallow angle, allowing it to peel back as the bread expands. Common reasons for no ear: scoring too deep or too straight down (score at a 30-degree angle), dough was over-proofed (less oven spring means less force to lift the ear), or the blade was not sharp enough (use a fresh razor blade, not a dull knife). Also make sure your oven is fully preheated — that initial blast of heat drives oven spring.

## Troubleshooting

### Q: Is the liquid on top of my starter mold?

Almost certainly not. That liquid is called hooch — it is alcohol produced by yeast when the starter has consumed all its food and is hungry. It can be gray, brown, or even black, and it will smell strongly of alcohol. Simply pour it off (or stir it back in for extra tang) and feed your starter. Mold looks very different: it appears as fuzzy spots (white, green, blue, pink, or orange) on the surface and is usually accompanied by a distinctly musty or rotten smell.

### Q: Why does my starter smell like nail polish remover?

That sharp acetone smell means your starter is producing a lot of acetic acid — it is very hungry and over-fermenting between feedings. This is not dangerous, just a cry for food. The fix: feed more frequently, increase the ratio of flour and water (try 1:2:2 instead of 1:1:1), and make sure the environment is not too warm. After 2-3 proper feedings, the smell should shift back to a pleasant, mild tang.

### Q: My starter doubled on Day 2, then stopped rising. Is it dead?

No, this is one of the most common experiences and it has a name: the Day 2-3 spike. The initial burst of activity comes from bacteria (primarily leuconostoc) that are already present in the flour. These bacteria produce gas but are not the right microbes for sourdough. Over the next few days, they die off and the true sourdough organisms (wild yeast and lactobacillus) slowly take over. Keep feeding on schedule — the real rise will return around Day 5-7.

### Q: Can I save a starter that has mold on it?

If you see actual fuzzy mold (not hooch), the safest answer is to start over. Mold can send invisible threads (hyphae) deep into the starter, and scraping off the surface does not guarantee you have removed it all. The good news: starting over only takes about a week. Some experienced bakers will scoop out a clean portion from the center and try to revive it, but for beginners, the risk of carrying mold into your bread is not worth it. Fresh flour is cheap. Start clean.

### Q: Why is my starter rising and falling really fast (within 2-3 hours)?

Your starter is very active and running out of food quickly. This is actually a good sign — it means you have a strong, healthy colony. The fix is simple: increase the ratio of fresh flour and water relative to the starter. Instead of 1:1:1, try 1:3:3 or even 1:5:5 in warm weather. This gives the yeast more food to work through and slows the cycle to a more manageable 6-8 hours, which also fits better with a once-or-twice-daily feeding schedule.

> **A final reassurance:** Sourdough baking has been done by ordinary people for thousands of years, long before kitchen scales, instant-read thermometers, and Instagram. Your first loaf does not need to be perfect. It needs to be baked. Every loaf teaches you something. Trust the process, trust your hands, and keep baking.
