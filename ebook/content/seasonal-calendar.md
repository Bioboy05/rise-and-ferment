# Seasonal Baking Calendar

Sourdough is alive, and like everything alive, it responds to the world around it. The same starter, the same flour, the same recipe can behave dramatically differently in July versus January. Understanding these seasonal shifts is what separates frustrated bakers from confident ones.

This calendar will help you adjust your process throughout the year so your results stay consistent no matter what the thermometer says.

---

## Spring (March -- May)

Spring is the season of waking up — for your garden, for your energy, and for your sourdough starter. As kitchen temperatures climb from the winter lows into the 18-24C (64-75F) range, you will notice your starter becoming more active, rising faster, and developing a livelier aroma.

**Feeding schedule adjustments.** Your starter is accelerating. If it was taking 10-12 hours to peak during winter, it may now peak in 6-8 hours. Watch it carefully and adjust your baking timeline accordingly. You may need to shift from one feed per day to two, especially as temperatures climb toward the end of May. Keep your standard 1:1:1 or 1:2:2 ratio for now — you will likely not need the aggressive ratios of summer yet.

**Fermentation changes.** Bulk fermentation times will start shortening. A dough that needed 6-8 hours in February might be ready in 4-6 hours by April. This is the most dangerous transition — if you stick to your winter timings without adjusting, you will over-ferment. Use visual and tactile cues (dough volume increase of 50-75%, jiggly and airy texture, visible bubbles on surface) rather than the clock.

**Best breads for spring.** This is an ideal time for herb-forward loaves. Fresh rosemary, thyme, chives, and wild garlic are coming into season. Try a roasted garlic and herb boule, or a spring onion focaccia. Lighter, moderately hydrated doughs (70-72%) are forgiving and satisfying as you recalibrate your timing.

**Common problems.** The biggest spring mistake is not realizing how much faster things are moving. If your bread suddenly starts coming out over-proofed (flat, dense crumb, no oven spring), your fermentation ran away from you. Shorten bulk time and check dough earlier than you think you need to.

**Seasonal additions.** Finely chopped fresh herbs, toasted sesame seeds, spring onion, sun-dried tomatoes, and cracked pepper all pair beautifully with the mild tang of spring sourdough.

> **Spring tip:** This is the perfect season to start a new starter from scratch. Moderate temperatures and increasing microbial activity in the environment give new starters the best chance of establishing quickly.

---

## Summer (June -- August)

Summer is both the most exciting and most challenging season for sourdough baking. Warm temperatures (25-35C / 77-95F) supercharge fermentation, which means everything happens faster — sometimes too fast. The bakers who thrive in summer are the ones who learn to slow things down.

**Managing fast fermentation.** Your starter may peak in 3-4 hours or less. Your dough may be fully fermented in 2-3 hours. If you are not paying attention, things will over-ferment before you even notice. The solution is aggressive feeding ratios: move from 1:1:1 to 1:3:3, 1:4:4, or even 1:5:5. This gives your starter much more food to work through and extends the cycle to a manageable length. Use cool water (18-20C / 64-68F) for feedings to further slow activity.

**The refrigerator is your best friend.** In summer, cold retarding is not optional — it is essential. Mix your dough in the late afternoon or evening, do a shorter bulk fermentation (3-4 hours), shape, and get the dough into the refrigerator as soon as possible. Bake straight from the fridge the next morning. The cold slows fermentation to a crawl and gives you full control over timing.

**Best breads for summer.** This is the season for flatbreads, pizza, focaccia, and grilled breads. They are quick, they do not heat up your kitchen for as long, and they showcase summer produce beautifully. If you are making a boule, bake early in the morning before the kitchen heats up.

**Warning signs of over-fermentation.** The dough feels slack and soupy rather than airy and strong. It spreads flat when you turn it out instead of holding its shape. The surface is covered in large, loose bubbles. It smells aggressively sour or boozy. If you catch it early, you can still shape and bake — it will just be denser and more sour than intended. If it has completely collapsed, turn it into focaccia or pizza dough where structure matters less.

**Seasonal additions.** Fresh basil, roasted red peppers, olives, caramelized onions, za'atar, and everything that screams Mediterranean summer. Tomato focaccia with fresh basil is one of the great pleasures of summer baking.

> **Summer tip:** If your kitchen regularly exceeds 30C (86F), consider doing your entire mix and bulk fermentation in the coolest room of your house, or even starting the bulk in the refrigerator from the beginning.

## Autumn (September -- November)

Welcome to the golden season of sourdough baking. Autumn is when everything clicks. Kitchen temperatures settle into the ideal 18-22C (64-72F) range, fermentation becomes predictable, and seasonal ingredients are at their absolute peak. If you are going to fall in love with sourdough, it will probably happen in October.

**Returning to standard schedules.** After the rapid-fire adjustments of summer, autumn lets you relax back into comfortable rhythms. A 1:1:1 or 1:2:2 feeding ratio works well again. Bulk fermentation times return to a predictable 5-7 hours. Your starter peaks in 6-8 hours. Everything feels dialed in.

**Building starter strength for winter.** Use autumn to get your starter into peak condition before the cold months. Feed it consistently, use high-quality flour, and consider incorporating some whole wheat or rye into feedings to boost microbial diversity and strength. A robust autumn starter will handle winter's slow pace much better than a neglected one.

**Harvest ingredients.** This is the season to go wild with inclusions. Toasted walnuts and honey. Cranberry and orange zest. Roasted pumpkin and sage. Kalamata olives and rosemary. Cinnamon raisin. Fig and anise. Seeded multigrain loaves packed with sunflower, flax, poppy, and pumpkin seeds. The pantry is overflowing — use it.

**Best breads for autumn.** Hearty, deeply crusted boules with complex flavor. This is the time for your most ambitious bakes: long cold retards for maximum tang, high-hydration open crumb experiments, multi-grain loaves packed with seeds and nuts. The moderate temperatures give you room for error and the long evenings give you time to enjoy the process.

**Common problems.** The main autumn issue is inconsistency as temperatures fluctuate. A warm October afternoon and a cold October morning can mean a 10C swing in your kitchen within the same day. If your results are inconsistent, start paying attention to the actual temperature in your kitchen when you mix, not just the season on the calendar.

> **Autumn tip:** This is the ideal season to build your sourdough confidence by baking 2-3 times per week. Consistent practice in forgiving conditions will teach you more than months of occasional baking.

## Winter (December -- February)

Winter slows everything down. Kitchen temperatures drop to 16-20C (61-68F) or lower, and your starter responds accordingly — rising sluggishly, taking longer to peak, and producing less lift. This is not a problem. It is an opportunity to develop deeper, more complex flavors through extended fermentation. But it does require patience and a few adjustments.

**Keeping your starter warm enough.** The single biggest winter challenge is maintaining adequate warmth for your starter and dough. Ideal fermentation temperature is 24-28C (75-82F), but many winter kitchens sit at 18C (64F) or lower. Strategies for warmth: place your starter near (not on) a radiator, on top of your refrigerator, inside your oven with just the light on (this adds about 5-8C), or in a microwave with a cup of hot water changed every few hours. A seedling heat mat set to 26C (78F) is a small investment that eliminates temperature worry entirely.

**Extended fermentation times.** Everything takes longer. A summer bulk that took 3-4 hours may take 8-12 hours in winter. Your starter may need 12-16 hours to peak on a 1:1:1 ratio. This is not a sign of weakness — it is normal cold-weather biology. Plan your bake schedule around these longer timelines. Many winter bakers mix their dough in the morning, do bulk fermentation all day, shape in the evening, cold retard overnight, and bake the next morning.

**Warmer water techniques.** Using warmer water (30-35C / 86-95F) for mixing your dough raises the final dough temperature and gives fermentation a head start. Aim for a final dough temperature of 24-26C (75-79F). Use this formula: target water temperature = (desired dough temp x 3) minus (flour temp + room temp + friction factor). Or simply use comfortably warm water and check the dough temperature with a thermometer after mixing.

**Best breads for winter.** This is the season for enriched doughs, holiday breads, and comfort baking. Cinnamon rolls with sourdough discard. Panettone. Chocolate babka. Brioche. Challah. Stollen. These richer doughs benefit from slower fermentation, and the butter, eggs, and sugar make them forgiving to work with. For standard loaves, whole wheat and rye blends develop magnificent depth of flavor in extended winter fermentation.

**Maintaining motivation when it is slow.** Winter sourdough requires trust. Your starter looks sluggish. Your dough barely seems to move. The temptation is to add commercial yeast "just to help." Resist. The slow fermentation is doing beautiful work — building complex organic acids, developing gluten gently over time, and creating flavors that fast fermentation cannot touch. Some of the best-tasting bread of the year comes from the slowest winter bakes.

> **Winter tip:** If you only bake once a week or less in winter, keep your starter in the fridge and pull it out 24-36 hours before you plan to bake. Give it 2-3 feedings with warm water in a warm spot to bring it back to full strength.

## Quick Reference Table

| Season | Ambient Temp | Feed Ratio | Water Temp | Peak Time | Tips |
|--------|-------------|-----------|-----------|----------|------|
| **Spring** | 18-24C (64-75F) | 1:1:1 to 1:2:2 | 26-28C (79-82F) | 6-8 hours | Watch for faster bulk; adjust timing weekly |
| **Summer** | 25-35C (77-95F) | 1:3:3 to 1:5:5 | 18-20C (64-68F) | 3-5 hours | Use fridge aggressively; bake early morning |
| **Autumn** | 18-22C (64-72F) | 1:1:1 to 1:2:2 | 26-28C (79-82F) | 6-8 hours | Best season to experiment and build skills |
| **Winter** | 14-20C (57-68F) | 1:1:1 | 30-35C (86-95F) | 10-16 hours | Find a warm spot; embrace the slow pace |

> **Year-round truth:** Temperature is the invisible ingredient in every sourdough recipe. Two bakers using the same flour, the same hydration, and the same method will get completely different results if their kitchens are at different temperatures. Learn to read your dough, not just the recipe, and you will bake consistently great bread in every season.
